## Key Items

- **Healthy Claim Definition** — finalized, 2028-02-28 (US-Federal)
    - Summary: FDA's updated 'healthy' claim rule (21 CFR 101.65) requires a food to fit a food-group model, not just hit nutrient thresholds. It was effective in 2025 with a compliance date of February 25, 2028.
    - Impacts: Any beverage that carries or implies 'healthy' must be re-checked against the new definition before the compliance date; adding vitamins to an unhealthy base no longer qualifies.
    - Implications (beverage industry): The industry loses a long-used marketing lever and must substantiate wellness positioning against a stricter federal standard.
    - Source: [FDA: Use of the Term Healthy on Food Labeling](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-food-labeling-and-critical-foods/use-term-healthy-food-labeling)
- **Synthetic Dye Phase-Out** — voluntary, 2026-12-31 (US-Federal)
    - Summary: Certified color additives are the synthetic FD&C dyes (Red 40, Yellow 5, Yellow 6, Blue 1, Blue 2, Green 3) listed and batch-certified by FDA. In 2025 FDA and HHS announced a push to phase out petroleum-based dyes, and FDA had already revoked the authorization for FD&C Red No. 3 in food in January 2025.
    - Impacts: Flavored and colored sparkling drinks using certified dyes face reformulation to exempt-from-certification colors; Red No. 3 is a hard removal item.
    - Implications (beverage industry): A color change is also a claims event for the industry: it can support or undercut a 'no artificial colors' statement, so color sourcing becomes a marketing decision, not just an R&D one.
    - Source: [FDA: Color Additives in Food](https://www.fda.gov/industry/color-additives/color-additives-and-foods)
- **Front-Of-Package Nutrition Labeling** — proposed, no date (US-Federal)
    - Summary: FDA proposed a front-of-package nutrition label (a 'Nutrition Info' box) rating Saturated Fat, Sodium, and Added Sugars as Low, Medium, or High on the front panel, amending 21 CFR Part 101.
    - Impacts: For a sweetened drink, added sugar is the most likely 'High' trigger and would reshape the front panel; a zero-sugar line is comparatively well positioned.
    - Implications (beverage industry): Front-of-package ratings could shift category-wide purchasing toward lower-sugar options and make added sugar a visible point-of-sale signal.
    - Source: [FDA: Front-of-Package Nutrition Labeling](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-facts-label/front-package-labeling)
- **GRAS Self-Affirmation Overhaul** — proposed, no date (US-Federal)
    - Summary: GRAS (Generally Recognized As Safe) lets a substance be added to food without FDA premarket approval if qualified experts agree it is safe; for decades a company could self-affirm GRAS and never tell FDA. FDA has signaled it will close that self-affirmation path and is re-reviewing older additives.
    - Impacts: Any ingredient a beverage relies on under self-affirmed GRAS may need a notification or removal; preservatives such as BHA and BHT are early re-review targets.
    - Implications (beverage industry): A beverage maker can no longer assume a self-affirmed ingredient is settled; the industry must hold audit-ready safety files for every additive in use.
    - Source: [FDA: How U.S. FDA's GRAS Notification Program Works](https://www.fda.gov/food/generally-recognized-safe-gras/how-us-fdas-gras-notification-program-works)
- **Caffeine Labeling Guidance** — in-progress, no date (US-Federal)
    - Summary: Caffeine added to cola-type beverages is GRAS up to 0.02 percent under 21 CFR 182.1180. FDA is working on voluntary caffeine-labeling guidance for the energy and functional categories and has warned about concentrated caffeine.
    - Impacts: A caffeinated line must keep added caffeine within the GRAS basis for its category and be ready for voluntary labeling expectations.
    - Implications (beverage industry): Energy and functional positioning carries added labeling and safety scrutiny, and intersects Canada's stricter supplemented-foods regime.
    - Source: [FDA: Spilling the Beans on Caffeine](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much)
- **Ultra-Processed Food Definition** — in-progress, no date (US-Federal)
    - Summary: There is no legal definition of 'ultra-processed food' yet; FDA and USDA announced they are jointly developing one. Once it exists it becomes a lever for warnings, school restrictions, and marketing pressure.
    - Impacts: Sweetened and heavily formulated beverages, especially those using non-nutritive sweeteners, are likely to land inside whatever definition emerges.
    - Implications (beverage industry): The industry faces a medium-term repositioning question; a definition could reshape claims, school access, and consumer perception across the category.
    - Source: [FDA: Human Foods Program updates](https://www.fda.gov/food/hfp-constituent-updates)
- **California Date Label Standardization** — enacted, 2026-07-01 (US-CA)
    - Summary: California AB 660 standardizes date labels: a date, if used, must use the 'BEST if Used By' or 'USE by' family of terms, with 'sell by' banned, effective July 1, 2026.
    - Impacts: Packaging copy on California-sold products must conform by July 1, 2026; this is a label-copy change, not a formula change.
    - Implications (beverage industry): Date-label standardization is spreading and reduces the flexibility brands have in package wording.
    - Source: [California Legislative Information: AB 660](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov)
- **California Food Safety Act Ingredient Ban** — enacted, 2027-01-01 (US-CA)
    - Summary: California AB 418, the California Food Safety Act, bans the manufacture or sale in California of foods containing brominated vegetable oil, potassium bromate, propylparaben, or FD&C Red No. 3, effective January 1, 2027. It parallels FDA's federal revocations of BVO (2024) and Red No. 3 (2025).
    - Impacts: Any California-sold formula containing BVO or Red No. 3 must be reformulated before January 1, 2027; given the federal revocations these are national removal items.
    - Implications (beverage industry): California sets the template other states copy, so its bans effectively become the national floor for the industry.
    - Source: [California Legislative Information: AB 418](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=202320240AB418)
- **California School Food Dye Ban** — enacted, 2027-12-31 (US-CA)
    - Summary: California AB 2316 bans six synthetic dyes from foods sold through school channels, effective December 31, 2027.
    - Impacts: Only products that reach the school channel are affected; core retail lines are not directly covered.
    - Implications (beverage industry): School channels are becoming a distinct, stricter market that the industry must formulate for separately.
    - Source: [California Legislative Information: AB 2316](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov)
- **California School Ultra-Processed Food Law** — enacted, 2028-06-01 (US-CA)
    - Summary: California AB 1264 is an enacted school-focused ultra-processed-food law; CDPH must define terms by June 1, 2028, and the definition names non-nutritive sweeteners such as sucralose.
    - Impacts: Sucralose-sweetened drinks in the school channel may be restricted once the definition is set.
    - Implications (beverage industry): California's UPF definition will likely become a reference other states and channels adopt.
    - Source: [California Legislative Information: AB 1264](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov)
- **Proposition 65 Warning Requirement** — enacted, no date (US-CA)
    - Summary: Proposition 65 is California's 1986 right-to-know law: it requires a warning when a product exposes a person to one of roughly 1,000 listed chemicals above a safe-harbor level. For beverages the drivers are trace heavy metals and acrylamide.
    - Impacts: Trace heavy metals carried in by botanicals, colors, concentrates, or water can trigger a warning obligation or a bounty-driven lawsuit; bottled water and botanical drinks carry the most exposure.
    - Implications (beverage industry): Exposure, not harm, triggers enforcement, so the industry carries ongoing testing and supplier-indemnity cost regardless of actual risk.
    - Source: [OEHHA: Proposition 65 list](https://oehha.ca.gov/proposition-65/proposition-65-list)
- **California Non-Ultraprocessed Certified Seal** — proposed, no date (US-CA)
    - Summary: California AB 2244, introduced February 2026, proposes a voluntary 'Non-Ultraprocessed Certified' seal with retailer good-faith identification, using a UPF definition tied to prior California law.
    - Impacts: Certification could be a shelf differentiator at large retailers; non-certified products may face a perception gap.
    - Implications (beverage industry): A voluntary seal can still pressure the whole category to reformulate to qualify.
    - Source: [California Legislative Information: AB 2244](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov)
- **California State GRAS Reporting Bill** — proposed, no date (US-CA)
    - Summary: A California bill would create a state-level GRAS review process, part of the broader state GRAS-reporting trend.
    - Impacts: Adds a state reporting and review burden on additives used in California-sold products.
    - Implications (beverage industry): A state-by-state GRAS patchwork would multiply compliance cost across the industry.
    - Source: [California Legislative Information](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov)
