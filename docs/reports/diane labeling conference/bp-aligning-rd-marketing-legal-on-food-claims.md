# bp-aligning-rd-marketing-legal-on-food-claims

A defensible food claim sits at the intersection of R&D evidence, marketing message, and legal risk, and the documented dossier is the product.

## Summary

Kantha Shelke (food scientist, Corvus Blue) and Ashish Talati (FDA/FTC labeling attorney) presented as a science-and-law team to argue that a claim is only defensible when the science supports it and the law can defend it at the same time. Their organizing image is three overlapping circles, R&D, marketing, and legal, whose intersection is the defensible claim. The repeated thesis is that if a claim is not documented, it is not defensible, so the claims register and substantiation dossier are the actual deliverable, not the label copy.

## Key Points

The bench-to-label translation gap is the core diagnostic. Every claim should clear four rungs before it reaches a label: a demonstrated ingredient mechanism, an in-product dose that is both effective and achievable in a serving, bioavailability in the actual food matrix, and a human-relevant clinical outcome in the target group. Most failures skip one or two rungs, producing recognizable failure modes such as relying on in vitro data alone, citing a surrogate biomarker as if it were a clinical outcome, formulating below the studied dose, ignoring the food matrix, leaning on an underpowered single trial, or mining a post-hoc subgroup.

The session toured the claim categories where this breaks down most often. Protein and satiety claims form a spectrum on the same product, from a defensible nutrient-content claim through a structure/function claim to a riskier functional-satiety claim, with PDCAAS and DIAAS quality scoring driving defensibility. Gut-health and immunity claims conflate distinct evidence bases, where strain does not equal species does not equal category, prebiotic does not equal fiber, and DSHEA structure/function language does not transfer to conventional foods. On express, implied, and net-impression claims, the FTC and courts judge the total package impression, fine print rarely cures a deceptive front panel, and the FTC Health Products Compliance Guidance is the operative document. Influencer and AI-generated marketing carry the same principle: if the brand benefits, the brand owns the substantiation, and a hallucinated study citation is a false-advertising exposure, so every external channel must route through the claims register.

The remaining segments covered the 2026 convergence landscape: the updated "healthy" rule shifting from a nutrient-threshold to a food-group model with the fortification rescue closed off, the front-of-pack nutrition info box, added-sugar and sugar-alternative gradients, the legally exposed "natural" and "clean" terms where the plaintiffs' bar sets the de facto standard, the bioengineered-disclosure interaction with "natural" marketing, the synthetic-dye phase-out and MAHA ingredient-ban wave, sustainability claims governed by aging FTC Green Guides plus state and EU rules, and the unregulated "GLP-1 friendly" category. A recurring cross-functional failure mode is the scale-up ingredient swap that updates a spec sheet without routing back to claim review, silently falsifying a "no artificial ingredients" claim.

## Takeaways

Three principles close the deck. Substantiation is simultaneously a science decision and a legal decision, and either bar alone fails. Regulatory belongs at the concept gate, not at mock-up review, because the costliest failures surface after science, copy, and packaging are already locked. And the dossier is the product: every channel, reformulation, and external message must trace to a substantiated entry, because undocumented means indefensible.
