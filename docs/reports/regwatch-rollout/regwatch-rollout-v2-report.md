## United States — FDA Food Safety (Beverage Focus)

### Healthy Claim Definition  
_finalized, effective 2028-02-28 · US-Federal_

**What happened.** FDA's updated 'healthy' claim rule redefines when a food may be labeled 'healthy': it must fit a food-group model, not just hit nutrient thresholds. The final rule is codified at 21 CFR 101.65; it was effective in 2025 with a compliance date in 2028.

**What it means.** Adding vitamins to an unhealthy base no longer rescues a 'healthy' claim. Any beverage that carries or implies 'healthy' must be re-checked against the new definition before the compliance date.

**Governing rule.** [21 CFR 101.65 (implied nutrient content claims, 'healthy')](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/part-101)

**Sources.** [FDA: Use of the Term Healthy on Food Labeling](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-food-labeling-and-critical-foods/use-term-healthy-food-labeling)

### Synthetic Dye Phase-Out  
_voluntary, effective 2026-12-31 · US-Federal_

**What happened.** Certified color additives are the synthetic FD&C dyes (Red 40, Yellow 5, Yellow 6, Blue 1, Blue 2, Green 3) listed and batch-certified by FDA. In 2025 FDA and HHS announced a push to phase out petroleum-based dyes, and FDA had already revoked the authorization for FD&C Red No. 3 in food in January 2025.

**What it means.** Flavored and colored sparkling drinks that use certified dyes face reformulation pressure to exempt-from-certification colors (fruit, vegetable, and mineral sources under 21 CFR Part 73). A color change is also a claims event: it can support or undercut a 'no artificial colors' statement, so it must route through claims review, not just R&D.

**Governing rule.** [21 CFR Part 74 (certified color additives)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/part-74); [21 CFR Part 73 (colors exempt from certification)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/part-73)

**Sources.** [FDA: Color Additives in Food](https://www.fda.gov/industry/color-additives/color-additives-and-foods) · [FDA: FD&C Red No. 3 revocation](https://www.fda.gov/food/hfp-constituent-updates)

### Front-Of-Package Nutrition Labeling  
_proposed · US-Federal_

**What happened.** FDA proposed a front-of-package nutrition label (a 'Nutrition Info' box) that would rate Saturated Fat, Sodium, and Added Sugars as Low, Medium, or High on the front panel. It amends the food-labeling regulations in 21 CFR Part 101.

**What it means.** For a sweetened drink, added sugar is the most likely 'High' trigger and would reshape the front panel. A zero-sugar line is comparatively well positioned, but any reformulation or redesign should be coordinated with this rule so the front panel is changed once, not twice.

**Governing rule.** [21 CFR 101.9 (nutrition labeling of food)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/part-101)

**Sources.** [FDA: Front-of-Package Nutrition Labeling](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-facts-label/front-package-labeling)

### GRAS Self-Affirmation Overhaul  
_proposed · US-Federal_

**What happened.** GRAS (Generally Recognized As Safe) is the status that lets a substance be added to food without FDA premarket approval if qualified experts agree it is safe. For decades a company could self-affirm GRAS and never tell FDA. FDA has signaled it will close that self-affirmation path and is re-reviewing older additives, several of which appear in beverages.

**What it means.** Any ingredient a beverage relies on under self-affirmed GRAS now needs a clean, audit-ready safety dossier. Preservatives such as BHA and BHT are early federal re-review targets. If FDA narrows the GRAS pathway, ingredients in use today could require a notification or a reformulation.

**Governing rule.** [21 CFR 170.30 (GRAS criteria)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/part-170); [21 CFR 170 Subpart E (GRAS notification)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/part-170)

**Sources.** [FDA: How U.S. FDA's GRAS Notification Program Works](https://www.fda.gov/food/generally-recognized-safe-gras/how-us-fdas-gras-notification-program-works)

### Caffeine Labeling Guidance  
_in-progress · US-Federal_

**What happened.** Caffeine added to cola-type beverages is GRAS up to 0.02 percent under 21 CFR 182.1180. FDA has separately warned about pure and highly concentrated caffeine and is working on voluntary caffeine-labeling guidance for the energy and functional categories.

**What it means.** A caffeinated line must keep added caffeine within the GRAS basis for its category and be ready for voluntary labeling expectations. Energy positioning also intersects Canada's supplemented-foods caution statements.

**Governing rule.** [21 CFR 182.1180 (caffeine GRAS)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/part-182)

**Sources.** [FDA: Spilling the Beans on Caffeine](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much)

### Ultra-Processed Food Definition  
_in-progress · US-Federal_

**What happened.** There is no legal definition of 'ultra-processed food' yet. FDA and USDA announced they are jointly developing one. There is no CFR text today; this is a watch item that, once defined, becomes a lever for warnings, school restrictions, and marketing pressure.

**What it means.** Sweetened and heavily formulated beverages are likely to land inside whatever definition emerges, especially products using non-nutritive sweeteners. It is not an immediate deadline but it shapes the medium-term reformulation and positioning strategy.

**Sources.** [FDA: Human Foods Program updates](https://www.fda.gov/food/hfp-constituent-updates)

### California Food Safety Act Ingredient Ban  
_enacted, effective 2027-01-01 · US-CA_

**What happened.** California AB 418, the California Food Safety Act, bans the manufacture or sale in California of foods containing brominated vegetable oil, potassium bromate, propylparaben, or FD&C Red No. 3, effective January 1, 2027. It is state law, not FDA, but it parallels federal action: FDA revoked BVO's food authorization in 2024 and Red No. 3's food authorization in 2025.

**What it means.** BVO and Red No. 3 are the concrete near-term beverage items. Any formula sold in California containing either must be out before January 1, 2027, and the federal revocations make removal a national, not just California, question.

**Governing rule.** [Federal parallel: BVO authorization revoked (former 21 CFR 180.30)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/part-180); [Federal parallel: FD&C Red No. 3 (21 CFR Part 74)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/part-74)

**Sources.** [California Legislative Information: AB 418](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=202320240AB418)

### Proposition 65 Warning Requirement  
_enacted · US-CA_

**What happened.** Proposition 65 is California's 1986 right-to-know law. It does not ban anything; it requires a warning when a product exposes a person to one of roughly 1,000 listed chemicals above a safe-harbor level. For beverages the drivers are trace heavy metals (lead, cadmium, arsenic, mercury) and acrylamide carried in by botanicals, colors, concentrates, or water.

**What it means.** Exposure, not harm, triggers a private enforcer's notice, and the burden shifts to the company to prove the exposure is below the safe-harbor level. Bottled water and botanical or fruit-forward beverages carry the most realistic heavy-metal exposure.

**Sources.** [OEHHA: Proposition 65 list](https://oehha.ca.gov/proposition-65/proposition-65-list)

## FDA Warning Letters — Enforcement Themes For Beverages

FDA enforces largely through warning letters; these recurring themes signal where attention lands. Live database: [FDA Warning Letters](https://www.fda.gov/inspections-compliance-enforcement-and-criminal-investigations/compliance-actions-and-activities/warning-letters).

### Unapproved drug and disease claims on functional beverages

**What triggers it.** Marketing a beverage with claims to treat, cure, or prevent disease (immunity, anxiety, sleep, weight loss) turns it into an unapproved new drug in FDA's view. Influencer and AI-drafted copy counts as the brand's claim.

### CBD and other unlawful additives in beverages

**What triggers it.** Adding CBD or other substances FDA has not authorized for food makes the beverage adulterated. FDA has issued many warning letters for CBD-infused drinks.

### Illegal or uncertified color additives

**What triggers it.** Using a color additive that is not listed for the use, not batch-certified where required, or used above its limit adulterates the product.

### Juice HACCP violations

**What triggers it.** Juice and juice-containing beverages must follow HACCP controls including a 5-log pathogen reduction; missing plans or controls draw letters.

### Acidified and low-acid beverage process controls

**What triggers it.** Shelf-stable acidified or low-acid beverages require filed processes and registration; failures in scheduled processes or registration draw letters.

### Preventive controls and facility registration (FSMA)

**What triggers it.** Failure to register a facility or to implement hazard analysis and risk-based preventive controls draws enforcement.

## Canada

### Canada Supplemented Foods Caution Statements  
_enacted · CA-Federal_

**What happened.** Health Canada's Supplemented Foods Regulations govern foods with added supplemental ingredients (including caffeine and certain vitamins). They require a Supplemented Food Facts table, cautionary statements, and caffeine limits, under Canada's Food and Drug Regulations rather than the US CFR.

**What it means.** A caffeinated or functional beverage sold in Canada may need cautionary statements and must stay within Canadian caffeine limits, which can differ from the US GRAS basis. This is the main Canadian touchpoint for the portfolio.

**Sources.** [Health Canada: Supplemented foods](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/food-nutrition/legislation-guidelines/supplemented-foods.html)

## Mexico

### Mexico NOM-051 Front-Of-Pack Warning Labels  
_enacted · MX-Federal_

**What happened.** Mexico's NOM-051 front-of-pack labeling requires black octagon warning seals for products high in calories, sugars, saturated fat, or sodium, plus disclaimer legends when a product contains non-nutritive sweeteners or added caffeine. It is Mexican federal law published in the Diario Oficial de la Federacion, not US CFR.

**What it means.** A sweetened or non-nutritive-sweetened beverage sold in Mexico likely carries one or more octagon seals and a sweetener disclaimer, and products with those seals face marketing-to-children restrictions. It is the strictest front-of-pack regime the portfolio meets.

**Sources.** [Diario Oficial de la Federacion](https://www.dof.gob.mx)
