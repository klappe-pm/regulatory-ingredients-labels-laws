# gensess-state-initiatives

State legislatures are driving a rapid, high-volume wave of food ingredient, GRAS, and UPF legislation, and industry advocacy is organizing to push for a unified federal standard.

## Summary

Dan Colegrove of the Food Ingredient Safety Coalition presented the state-legislative landscape from an industry-advocacy vantage point. The deck argues that perceived federal inaction has emboldened states to legislate quickly and in large volume, that the food and beverage industry has lost public trust, and that a coordinated, science-based federal approach is preferable to a costly patchwork. Because the source is a coalition position deck, the framing reflects FISC's advocacy posture.

## Key Points

Colegrove opened on why state legislatures are effective venues for this activity: independence to enact their own food laws, speed from annual sessions, the sheer volume of bills that can move quickly, and federal inaction that emboldens action. He framed a trust crisis in which the industry is increasingly treated like Big Tobacco, attributing it to perceived federal inaction, mistrust of scientific institutions, social-media influencers, agenda-driven organizations, MAHA popularity, and a slow-to-respond industry. The volume trajectory is stark: in 2023 only three bills across two states with one new law, California's AB 418, versus 2025-2026 with more than 140 bills filed across 42 states and new laws in thirteen.

The legislation clusters into school food restrictions, state GRAS reporting, ultra-processed-food definitions, and statewide bans and labeling, targeting more than 100 ingredients including all synthetic colors, potassium bromate, titanium dioxide, sweeteners such as aspartame, propylparaben, BHA and BHT, vanillin, high-fructose corn syrup, seed oils including soy and safflower, brominated flour, diacetyl, trans fats, brominated vegetable oil, and carrageenan. School-food laws have passed in Iowa, Nebraska, and Tennessee, with potassium bromate and propylparaben frequently referenced, and Colegrove noted lawmakers find it hard to appear unsympathetic to children's health. State GRAS bills are active in California, New Jersey, and New York and are animated by a "GRAS loophole" narrative. UPF definition efforts are led by California, state bans face challenges illustrated by West Virginia litigation, and labeling bills have been slowed by Texas litigation even as California's AB 2244 non-UPF certification plan advances. He highlighted AB 2244 specifically as a voluntary state "Non-Ultraprocessed Certified" seal that legislators hope becomes a national standard.

He argued that inaccuracies persist in legislative debate, citing claims that substances are banned in Europe but not here, that they are deadly or known health risks, and that big food exploits the GRAS loophole to engineer addictive food. Looking ahead, he expects the issues to return in 2027 as a post-election year favors aggressive action, with much riding on whether Washington acts. He closed with a business-engagement playbook spanning testimony, written comments, local lobbyists, media support, sponsored research, state coalitions, grassroots tools, and ally coordination, and with FISC's positions that the US has the safest and most affordable food system, that a patchwork of state laws is costly, that states lack the needed expertise and resources, and that a unified science-based national approach is critical.

## Takeaways

The operative signal for labelers is that ingredient scrutiny is now permanent and multi-jurisdictional regardless of whether any single bill survives challenge, so compliance must assume a moving target across school-food, GRAS-disclosure, UPF-definition, and outright-ban models simultaneously. The deck is an advocacy position; treat its trust-crisis framing and "bad science" characterizations as the coalition's argument rather than neutral analysis.
