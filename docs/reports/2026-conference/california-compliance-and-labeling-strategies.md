# california-compliance-and-labeling-strategies

California's stringent, fast-moving, state-specific food laws and its pro-plaintiff Proposition 65 regime require their own compliance playbook.

## Summary

Jennifer Gross and Nicole Bayne of OFW Law walked through why California compliance warrants separate treatment: unique state-specific rules, frequent legislative updates, industry-wide impact, and broad national reach because most national brands ship into the state. The session covered food and color additive bans, standardized date labeling, ultra-processed-food legislation, and an extended treatment of Proposition 65 liability for food. The deck carries an explicit disclaimer that it is informational and not legal advice.

## Key Points

On additive and ingredient bans, AB 418 (the California Food Safety Act) makes it unlawful from January 1, 2027 to manufacture, sell, distribute, or hold food containing brominated vegetable oil, potassium bromate, propylparaben, or Red Dye 3, with civil penalties up to $5,000 for a first violation and $10,000 for subsequent ones. AB 2316 bars six synthetic dyes (Blue 1, Blue 2, Green 3, Red 40, Yellow 5, Yellow 6) from competitive foods sold during school hours starting December 31, 2027, with narrow fundraising exceptions. A newer bill, AB 2034, would require manufacturers to notify the California Department of Public Health before using a new additive, submitting GRAS-equivalent evidence for a state license, with a July 1, 2027 compliance target.

On date labeling, AB 660 standardizes voluntary date labels effective July 1, 2026: "BEST if Used By" for quality and "USE by" for safety, with two-letter acronyms for small packages, and it prohibits the term "sell by," with non-compliance exposed to a misdemeanor charge. On ultra-processed foods, AB 1264 defines UPF by technical-effect ingredients plus high saturated fat, sodium, or added sugar, directs CDPH to define "ultraprocessed foods of concern" and "restricted school foods" by mid-2028, and phases those out of school meals by 2035, while the speakers flagged that there is no scientific consensus on what "ultra-processed" means. A companion bill, AB 2244, would create a voluntary state "Non-Ultraprocessed Certified" seal.

The Proposition 65 segment was the deepest. Prop 65 is a right-to-know warning statute, not a ban, whose chemical list has grown from roughly 30 substances in 1987 to about 1,000, and food's share of total notices has climbed sharply, with cadmium notices surging in recent quarters alongside lead and acrylamide. The incentive structure is unusual and pro-plaintiff: the plaintiff need only show the chemical is present, after which the defendant bears the burden of proving the exposure falls below a safe harbor, many listed chemicals have no safe harbor at all, exposure analysis is expert-intensive and expensive, and every party in the supply chain is potentially liable. Safe harbors derive from the No Significant Risk Level for carcinogens and the Maximum Allowable Dose Level for reproductive toxicants, both set very low. Food-specific defenses and disputes include frequency-of-consumption exposure analysis and the naturally-occurring defense, illustrated by the Beech-Nut and Tri-Union Seafoods cases. Procedurally, a plaintiff must serve a 60-day notice with a Certificate of Merit before suing, most notices never mature into lawsuits, and early settlement economics favor plaintiffs because there is no court oversight of fees. Defensive measures include broad warnings, annual testing, supplier and buyer contracting, indemnity, and drafting settlements to be expansive while keeping admitted liability restrictive.

## Takeaways

California operates as a de facto national floor for additive and labeling compliance, and brands should audit "healthy," "no added sugar," and additive exposure together rather than in separate workstreams. Prop 65's burden-shifting and lack of safe harbors make food, including some of the healthiest categories, a standing litigation target, so annual testing, supplier indemnity, and warning discipline are the operative protections.
