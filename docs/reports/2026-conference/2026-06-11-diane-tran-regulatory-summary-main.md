# 01-regulatory-landscape-summary

In one sentence: Food and beverage companies can no longer treat compliance as "federal or state" — they now face simultaneous, sometimes conflicting demands from both, and the answer is a single unified ingredient-and-labeling playbook built for readiness rather than prediction.

## What The Session Covered

This is the opening session of the conference, delivered by a food-and-drug attorney from Akin Gump. It sets the stage for everything that follows by mapping the current regulatory environment: what the federal government (FDA and Congress) is doing on ingredients and labels, what individual states are doing on their own, and where those two layers collide and create legal uncertainty and product-liability risk. It closes with a practical eight-step framework for building one coordinated compliance strategy.

The deck is a full narrative presentation with substantive legal content, not a header-only outline. It covers FDA's baseline authority, FDA's 2026 priorities, pending federal legislation, a wave of new state laws (Texas, New York, California, Louisiana, Arizona, West Virginia and others), the four constitutional "collision points" between state and federal rules, and a step-by-step playbook.

## Key Points

- The core thesis: the compliance question has shifted from "federal or state" to "both, now." Companies must satisfy federal rules and a growing patchwork of state rules at the same time.
- FDA's baseline authority comes from the "misbranding" rule. A food is "misbranded" if its label does not meet federal standards, and misbranded food legally cannot be shipped across state lines. Mandatory label elements include the statement of identity (the product's common name), the full ingredient list in order of predominance, net quantity, the manufacturer/distributor name and address, and the Nutrition Facts panel (required since the NLEA of 1990).
- Claims are regulated by type: nutrient-content claims (such as "low fat" or "high fiber") must match FDA-defined definitions; health claims must be FDA-authorized; structure/function claims are allowed but governed by different standards.
- Ingredient safety rests on the 1958 Food Additives Amendment: any substance added to food must be pre-approved by FDA unless it is GRAS. FALCPA (2004) requires labeling of major allergens.
- Federal preemption (NLEA Section 403A) generally blocks states from imposing labeling requirements that are "not identical to" federal ones in defined areas (identity, nutrition labeling, nutrient-content and health claims, and more). This is the legal shield companies have historically relied on for one national label.
- The critical exception: safety warnings are NOT preempted. The NLEA expressly does not block state requirements for a warning about the safety of a food or a food component. This single carve-out is what opens the door to the current state warning-label wave.
- FDA's 2026 priorities split into two tracks. Food chemical safety: making GRAS notification mandatory instead of voluntary, post-market chemical reviews, contaminant action levels, natural color additive work, and caffeine/allergen labeling. Nutrition and labeling: Front-of-Package (FOP) labeling work, a new "healthy" claim definition, added-sugar and sodium reduction targets, and online grocery labeling. Compliance timelines run roughly 3 to 4 years where final label rules apply.
- GRAS reform is moving from voluntary to mandatory. A proposed FDA rule would require companies to submit GRAS notices rather than self-affirm in private, and would clarify how FDA decides a substance is not GRAS. Multiple bills are pending in Congress (H.R. 4958, H.R. 7291, S. 2341, S. 3122) pushing notification and reassessment requirements.
- FDA has finalized a post-market assessment program for chemicals already in food and launched reassessments of specific additives (BHT and ADA named), backed by a public prioritization tool. The direction is from one-time disclosure toward ongoing, systematic ingredient governance.
- The state patchwork is real and active, not hypothetical:
  - Texas SB 25 targets 44 ingredients with a warning-label mandate (deadline January 2027). It is being challenged on First Amendment compelled-speech grounds, federal preemption, vagueness, and the Commerce Clause; a court in the Western District of Texas granted an injunction.
  - New York S1239F would create a state-level GRAS disclosure database (mandatory reporting of self-affirmed GRAS, publicly searchable) and ban Red 3, potassium bromate, and propylparaben. It passed both chambers and awaits the governor.
  - Louisiana SB 14 requires QR-code ingredient disclosures plus school-food restrictions. Arizona, California, Utah, Virginia, and West Virginia have UPF definitions, school-food rules, or retail ingredient bans. Seventeen-plus states are exploring SNAP purchase limitations.
- State approaches are diverging deliberately: some ban ingredients, some require disclosure, some restrict distribution channels (schools, SNAP). A company can be hit by several different regulatory models at once.
- Four "collision points" between federal and state law: preemption scope (state ingredient bans versus federal GRAS status), the First Amendment (compelled-speech doctrine and whether mandated warnings get rational-basis or strict-scrutiny review), the Commerce Clause (state-by-state labels burdening interstate commerce), and the underlying fight over who decides safety — FDA versus state legislatures versus courts, science versus political will.
- The recommended playbook is eight steps: (1) build one master ingredient and claims inventory; (2) classify each item's regulatory status; (3) map triggers by ingredient function, not just by name; (4) create a label-change control gate; (5) keep GRAS and post-market files ready; (6) segment each item into reformulate, relabel, restrict, or defend; (7) update supplier contracts and attestations; (8) align litigation, regulatory, science, and communications teams.
- The five closing takeaways: keep one inventory rather than multiple trackers; treat GRAS documentation as a regulatory asset that protects against both federal and state risk; plan for both reformulation and legal uncertainty (litigation may delay laws, but ingredient scrutiny is permanent); use label-change moments strategically by coordinating FOP, "healthy," and state warnings together; and aim for readiness rather than prediction.

## What It Means For A Beverage Business

- A non-alcoholic sparkling-beverage maker is squarely in scope. Beverages carry ingredient lists, Nutrition Facts panels, claims, colors, and additives — every lever this session discusses.
- The safety-warning carve-out is the key exposure. Even though one national label is normally protected by preemption, state safety warnings are not preempted, so a beverage sold nationally can still get pulled into Texas SB 25-style warning mandates if any of its ingredients land on a state list.
- One of the session's own discussion scenarios is a beverage: a drink using a self-affirmed GRAS ingredient with no FDA notification, sold in New York, would face NY S1239F disclosure-database exposure if that bill is enacted, plus exposure to federal GRAS reform. If Sparkling Ice or any product relies on self-affirmed GRAS ingredients, those dossiers need to be audit-ready now.
- Color and sweetener choices carry direct risk. Banned or targeted additives in the state bills (Red 3, potassium bromate, propylparaben, and the broader 44-ingredient Texas list) plus the federal natural-color-additive work mean colorants and "no artificial colors" style claims need verification against both federal listings and diverging state definitions of "artificial."
- FOP labeling, the new "healthy" definition, and added-sugar/sodium targets all matter to beverages and share the same physical label real estate as any state warning. The session's advice to coordinate label changes into one planned moment (rather than reacting piecemeal) is the operational takeaway: pair any packaging refresh with FOP, claims, and state-warning readiness at once.
- Practical first move: a single cross-functional ingredient-and-claims inventory mapped by function, with GRAS files ready and supplier attestations updated — not separate trackers owned by R&D, legal, and regulatory in isolation.

# 02-fda-policy-update-summary

In one sentence: A senior FDA nutrition advisor walks through the agency's 2026 food agenda, where the headline moves are front-of-package nutrition labels, a crackdown on ultra-processed foods and artificial colors, closing the GRAS self-certification loophole, and rewriting decades-old product standards.

## What The Session Covered

Laura Carroll of FDA's Human Foods Program (the unit that runs nutrition and food safety) gave a status update on FDA policy as of mid-2026. The deck is a real substantive briefing, not just an outline. It walked through three areas: FDA's role in nutrition, the new Dietary Guidelines for Americans, and FDA's 2026 priority list. The throughline is the "Make America Healthy Again" (MAHA) agenda, the current administration's push to reduce diet-related chronic disease by reshaping the food supply and giving consumers clearer information.

FDA frames its job two ways: empower consumers to spot healthier choices quickly, and help build a healthier food supply. FDA regulates roughly 80 percent of the US food supply (USDA handles most meat and poultry), so its rules reach nearly every packaged-food and beverage company.

## Key Points

- The 2025-2030 Dietary Guidelines for Americans are the nutrition backdrop for the deck, with the cover emphasizing vegetables and fruits, whole grains, protein, dairy, and healthy fats. These guidelines inform what counts as "healthy" and shape future labeling rules.
- FDA's 2026 Human Foods Program priorities sit in three buckets: nutrition, food chemical safety, and microbiological food safety.
- Ultra-processed foods (UPFs): a growing body of research links UPFs to negative health outcomes, and addressing them is central to MAHA. FDA and USDA are jointly developing a single uniform definition of "ultra-processed." There is no agreed definition yet; once issued, it will determine which products get labeled or scrutinized.
- Front-of-package (FOP) nutrition labeling: FDA is moving toward a standardized front-label box showing saturated fat, sodium, and added sugars, each rated Low, Medium, or High. This puts a simplified at-a-glance nutrition judgment on the front of the package, not just the detailed Nutrition Facts panel on the back.
- Allergen labeling: FDA held a public meeting on food allergen thresholds (the amount of an allergen that actually triggers a reaction) in February 2026, and issued a January 2026 request for information on gluten labeling and preventing gluten cross-contact in packaged foods.
- Standards of Identity (SOI): FDA is reviewing its portfolio of 250-plus product definitions. In July 2025 it moved to revoke or propose revoking 52 outdated standards, and in August 2025 proposed updating the 60-plus-year-old standard for pasteurized orange juice.
- Additional nutrition priorities named: caffeine labeling guidance, an added-sugars reduction strategy, implementation of the updated "healthy" claim, and sodium reduction.
- Food chemical safety: FDA is moving to eliminate petroleum-based (synthetic) food dyes. It has started revoking authorization for two such dyes, approved new colors from natural sources, launched a public tracker of company commitments, and is exercising enforcement discretion for "no artificial colors" claims.
- FDA developed a proposed rule to close the "GRAS loophole" — the practice where companies can self-certify a new ingredient as Generally Recognized As Safe without notifying FDA.
- FDA finalized a post-market chemical assessment program (a systematic process for re-checking the safety of additives already on the market) and launched reassessments of BHT (a preservative for fats and oils) and azodicarbonamide (ADA, used in flour and bread).

## What It Means For A Beverage Business

- Front-of-package labeling is the most direct hit if finalized. A standardized FOP box rating added sugars and sodium as Low/Med/High would appear on sparkling-beverage packaging. Zero-sugar and zero-sodium sparkling water would likely earn favorable "Low" ratings, which is a marketing advantage; any sweetened or sodium-containing line could draw a "Med" or "High" flag on the front of the can or bottle.
- The added-sugars reduction strategy and the updated "healthy" claim define which products can carry positive nutrition messaging. Unsweetened sparkling lines are well positioned to qualify; sweetened variants may not.
- Caffeine labeling guidance matters for any caffeinated or energy-positioned sparkling product. Expect clearer disclosure expectations as FDA develops guidance.
- Artificial color elimination is a formulation issue. Any synthetic dye used for flavor variants is now on a clock; switching to natural-source colors affects shade, stability, and cost. The "no artificial colors" enforcement discretion is also a clean marketing opening for products that are already dye-free.
- The GRAS loophole closure and post-market chemical reassessments (BHT, ADA, and others to come) raise scrutiny on novel sweeteners, flavor systems, preservatives, and processing aids. Ingredient and supplier choices that relied on self-certified GRAS status may need stronger safety documentation.
- The ultra-processed-food definition is a watch item. Depending on how FDA and USDA draw the line, sweetened and heavily formulated beverages could fall inside the UPF category, with labeling or reformulation pressure to follow. The definition does not exist yet, so the move now is to track it, not react.
- Standards of Identity review is mostly a juice and dairy story (the orange-juice update is the named example), but it signals FDA willingness to rewrite long-settled product definitions, which can reach flavored and fruit-based beverage categories.

# 03-maha-and-the-administration-summary

In one sentence: A government-affairs briefing on how the Make America Healthy Again movement and the current administration are reshaping federal food policy in 2026, with FDA and USDA pushing front-of-package labeling, ingredient and additive crackdowns, an ultra-processed-foods definition, added-sugar reduction, and state-level SNAP bans that directly target sweetened beverages.

## What The Session Covered

This was a regulatory-intelligence session delivered by Jared Rothstein of AgencyIQ (the regulatory-analysis arm of Politico). It walked through the political backdrop, the leadership shake-up at the Food and Drug Administration, the agency's near-term to-do list, and a set of specific rules and programs that will hit food and beverage companies over the next year. The throughline: a turbulent year of staff turnover and informal policymaking, but a remarkably consistent agenda aimed at chronic diet-related disease, "radical transparency" about ingredients, and pressure on sugar, additives, and ultra-processed foods.

The deck moved through five blocks: what MAHA is and who leads it; FDA's leadership outlook; FDA's topline priorities (deliverables, rules, and guidances on the horizon); upcoming agency initiatives (front-of-package labeling, the ultra-processed-foods definition, the GRAS rule, color additives, dietary guidelines, and SNAP beverage restrictions); and final thoughts on funding and the political road to the midterms.

## Key Points

- MAHA is a health-and-food movement organized around Robert F. Kennedy Jr. (RFK Jr.), who runs the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS), FDA's parent department. Its food agenda: cut chronic diet-related disease (especially in children), reduce food-and-ag corporate influence, remove "harmful" additives and chemicals (food dyes, pesticides), push "real food" over ultra-processed food, rewrite nutrition guidelines (more saturated fat and animal protein, less added sugar and refined carbs), and pursue "radical transparency" through labeling, testing, and premarket review.
- Leadership is unsettled. FDA Commissioner Marty Makary is out; Kyle Diamantas is in, likely as acting commissioner possibly through year-end. Don Prater stays as acting deputy leading the Human Foods Program (HFP), the FDA office that regulates food. The food-side leadership is described as comparatively stable even amid the broader churn, which means the food agenda keeps moving regardless of the top-job uncertainty.
- Front-of-package (FOP) nutrition labeling is the marquee item. A Biden-era proposal would have required an interpretive "Nutrition Info" box on the front of packages flagging added sugar, saturated fat, and sodium as Low/Med/High. The current administration signals a substantial rewrite: a possible "traffic light" green/yellow/red color scheme instead of the black-and-white box, less focus on saturated fat, and possible inclusion of nutrients to encourage (such as fiber). Big changes raise the prospect of a fresh proposed rule and a longer timeline.
- An ultra-processed-foods (UPF) definition is being drafted but is not settled. UPF generally means industrially formulated products with ingredients you would not use in a home kitchen. A federal request for public comment drew over 19,000 submissions (5,000+ posted). Commenters fought over the NOVA classification system, pushed to anchor any definition in nutrition-facts and ingredient-list data, and asked for carve-outs (supplements, medical foods, infant formula, fresh produce). There is no committed timeline or output, and Kennedy has linked the UPF concept to the FOP labeling rule.
- The GRAS rule is headed for "scaled-down" reform. GRAS ("Generally Recognized as Safe") is the pathway by which companies can self-determine an ingredient is safe and add it to food without formal FDA pre-approval. HHS and FDA want to "close the GRAS loophole." Options range from simply requiring companies to notify FDA of self-affirmed determinations (lighter touch) to full mandatory pre-market submission. The proposal has sat under White House regulatory review (OIRA) since December 1; the open question is whether it applies only to new ingredients (forward-looking) or also to ingredients already on the market (retrospective).
- The synthetic dye phaseout is advancing toward an end-of-2026 deadline. FDA is offering enforcement flexibility for voluntary "no artificial colors" claims if a product contains no FD&C (synthetic certified) colors, has approved new natural colors (beetroot red, spirulina extract; six natural approvals total), and signaled it may tighten impurity/heavy-metal limits on natural colors and build a testing system for them.
- Post-market chemical reviews are ramping. FDA finalized a framework to systematically reassess chemicals already in the food supply; BHA, BHT, and ADA are named next high-priority assessment targets, while the 2026 priority list also references phthalates and propylparaben. Self-affirmed GRAS substances could be pulled into this if retrospective reporting is required. The agency notes staffing and resource limits as a real constraint.
- Added-sugar reduction is a defined strategy. FDA plans a voluntary, industry-focused sugar-reduction framework modeled on its sodium-reduction targets, is exploring a new "low added sugar" nutrient content claim, and is reviewing labeling for sugar alternatives and non-nutritive sweeteners, plus consumer-education efforts.
- The 2025-2030 Dietary Guidelines for Americans (DGAs) emphasize whole, nutrient-dense, minimally processed foods, animal protein, and dairy, while warning against ultra-processed foods and added sugars. They drive federal nutrition standards and procurement (military, VA, prisons, school meals, WIC) and may push FDA to revise its "healthy" nutrient-content claim.
- SNAP beverage restrictions are the most direct beverage hit. SNAP (the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, formerly food stamps) is federal grocery assistance. USDA has approved waivers letting 23 states bar SNAP dollars from buying specific products, with restrictions taking effect in 10 states (many in 2026). The restricted lists overwhelmingly target soda, soft drinks, sugar-sweetened/sweetened beverages, energy drinks, and "drinks with less than 50% natural juice." Enforcement (FNS monitoring and test purchases) begins 90 days after a state's implementation date; a lawsuit by SNAP recipients seeks to invalidate the waivers.
- Other watch items: expanded allergen labeling (including gluten), the Food Traceability Rule compliance date pushed to July 20, 2028, a shift to risk-based and state-partner food-safety inspections (FDA's "BRIDGE" modernization), a House FY2027 funding bill giving FDA $7.1 billion (HFP $1.28 billion), and an FDA legislative wish list to Congress (27 proposals, 5 touching food) seeking expanded contaminant authority and mandatory industry chemical-data submissions.
- Bottom line from the speaker: expect big policy moves announced informally (outside formal rulemaking), continued leadership instability, a growing tension between MAHA's agenda and the administration's need to keep food affordable before the midterms, and FDA looking for visible "wins" (dye phaseout, infant-formula work, GRAS reform, faster chemical reviews) to show progress.

## What It Means For A Beverage Business

- SNAP beverage bans are the clearest near-term exposure. Sparkling Ice is zero-sugar and uses non-nutritive sweeteners, so it should sit outside the soda / sugar-sweetened-beverage targets in most state lists. But several state definitions are broad ("sweetened beverages," "sweetened, carbonated beverages," "carbonated water and certain sugars as the first two ingredients" in Tennessee). Whether a zero-sugar sweetened sparkling water is "in" depends on each state's exact wording. This is worth a product-by-product, state-by-state read before retrofitting nothing or assuming exemption.
- Front-of-package labeling is the single biggest design and brand variable. A shift from a black-and-white Low/Med/High box to a green/yellow/red "traffic light" scheme would change front-of-pack real estate and the visual story for every SKU. A zero-sugar, low-sodium sparkling beverage likely scores well under any interpretive scheme, which is a marketing opportunity. Track whether the rule is re-proposed (resetting the timeline) and which nutrients get flagged versus encouraged (fiber inclusion could matter for functional lines).
- Sweeteners are under the microscope. FDA's added-sugar strategy explicitly includes reviewing labeling for sugar alternatives and non-nutritive sweeteners. Brands built on non-nutritive sweeteners should expect more scrutiny of sweetener claims and possibly a "low added sugar" claim definition to align to. The DGAs' anti-added-sugar stance is a tailwind for zero-sugar positioning but a watch item for sweetener perception.
- A "no artificial colors" claim opportunity exists now. FDA's enforcement-discretion policy lets products with no FD&C synthetic colors use claims like "no artificial colors." If the beverage line is already free of synthetic dyes, this is a low-cost MAHA-aligned claim, paired with the approved natural color options (beetroot red, spirulina) if color is needed.
- GRAS and post-market chemical reviews could touch the ingredient deck. Flavors, preservatives, color sources, and functional additives that rely on self-affirmed GRAS status may face new notification or reporting requirements, and named post-market targets (BHA, BHT) plus impurity limits on natural colors could reach beverage formulations. Inventory which ingredients depend on self-affirmed GRAS now, before any retrospective requirement lands.
- A UPF definition is a latent reputational and labeling risk. If a federal UPF definition keys off ingredient lists or sweeteners, formulated beverages could be swept in regardless of sugar content, and UPF status may eventually connect to front-of-pack labeling. No timeline yet, but it is a strategic item for R&D and brand to watch, not a near-term compliance task.
- Expect informal, fast-moving signals. Much policy is being announced outside formal rulemaking, so the practical advice is to monitor agency statements and state SNAP actions continuously rather than waiting for final rules.

# 04-state-initiatives-summary

In one sentence: With the federal government slow to act, states have exploded from a handful of ingredient bills in 2023 to over 140 bills across 42 states in 2025-2026 that ban or restrict specific food and beverage ingredients, demand new safety reporting, define ultraprocessed food, and add label warnings, creating a patchwork that any beverage maker selling nationally must track.

## What The Session Covered

Dan Colegrove of the Food Ingredient Safety Coalition (FISC), an industry advocacy group, walked through the rapid rise of state-level food and beverage regulation. The talk frames why states have become the main battleground (legislatures are fast, independent, and emboldened by federal inaction), the categories of bills being filed, the specific ingredients targeted, and how industry is organizing to respond. The presenter's perspective is openly pro-industry: FISC's stated position is that the U.S. food supply is already safe and that a state-by-state patchwork is costly and unscientific.

A central backdrop is the collapse of public trust in the food industry. The deck cites a 2021 Newsweek "Toxic Food" cover and the MAHA ("Make America Healthy Again") movement as signs the industry is now treated "like Big Tobacco," with social media influencers and advocacy organizations driving much of the legislative momentum.

## Key Points

- Trajectory of activity. In 2023, only three bills were filed in two states (California and New Jersey), producing one new law. By 2025-2026, over 140 bills were filed in 42 states, with new laws enacted in 13 states. The presenter says the volume exceeded predictions.
- California led. California Assembly Bill 418 (2023) was the first to pass. It bans products containing any of four ingredients starting in 2027. The California compliance session identifies those four as brominated vegetable oil, potassium bromate, propylparaben, and Red Dye No. 3.
- Over 100 ingredient targets across all bills. Named targets include: all synthetic colors (FD&C dyes), potassium bromate, titanium dioxide, sweeteners including aspartame, propylparaben, BHA and BHT, vanillin, high fructose corn syrup, seed oils including soy and safflower, brominated flour, diacetyl, trans fats, brominated vegetable oil (BVO), and carrageenan.
- Bills fall into five categories: school food restrictions, state GRAS reporting, ultraprocessed food (UPF) definitions, statewide bans and labeling, and label warnings.
- School foods. New laws in Iowa, Nebraska, and Tennessee; proposed bills cover most or all FD&C colors, and potassium bromate and propylparaben are frequently named. School nutritionists have become active. The presenter notes it is politically hard for lawmakers to look unsympathetic to children's health, so these bills are difficult to stop.
- State GRAS bills. Active debates in California, New Jersey, and New York. These would create new state-level review bureaucracies and, per FISC, raise consumer and business costs. The driving narrative is the "GRAS loophole" — the claim that companies exploit an outdated self-certification system to push harmful chemicals.
- UPF definitions. Definitions vary state to state. California is attempting to define UPF; absent a federal definition, states will keep writing their own, which FISC frames as a core problem.
- State bans. States can ban product sales outright. West Virginia litigation illustrates legal challenges to such bans. The list of targeted substances shifts with social media attention, including substances the presenter argues are flagged on "bad science."
- Labeling legislation. Texas litigation has slowed some label bills, but they keep reappearing. California's "Non-UPF" plan is advancing.
- Focus bill — California AB 2244 (introduced February 2026). Creates a voluntary state certification program with a "Non-Ultraprocessed Certified" seal, using a UPF definition tied to prior California law. Large retailers would have to make good-faith efforts to clearly identify certified items. California legislators intend it as a trend-setting model they hope becomes a national standard.
- Looking ahead. FISC says it has largely succeeded in stopping "misguided" bills so far, but expects the issues to return in 2027, with the first year after an election being a window for aggressive action. Continued federal inaction is expected to fuel state activity.
- Industry response. FISC coordinates in-committee and virtual testimony, written comments, local lobbyists, media support, sponsored research, state coalitions, grassroots activation, and coordination with allies.

## What It Means For A Beverage Business

- Sparkling beverages are squarely in scope. Several named targets are common in flavored and functional drinks: synthetic colors, aspartame and other sweeteners, BVO (historically a citrus-flavor emulsifier), carrageenan, and high fructose corn syrup. Even a "clean" formulation should be screened against the current target list, because a single banned ingredient can block sale of an entire SKU in a state.
- Reformulation deadlines are real and dated. California AB 418's four-ingredient ban takes effect in 2027. A nationally distributed product generally has to meet the strictest state's rule or maintain separate formulations, so the most aggressive state effectively sets the floor.
- A patchwork raises cost and complexity. Differing UPF definitions, GRAS reporting duties, and label-warning rules across dozens of states mean a beverage maker may face different obligations per state for the same product. Track which states have enacted versus merely proposed bills.
- Marketing and brand exposure. A "Non-Ultraprocessed Certified" seal (California AB 2244) could become a shelf differentiator at large retailers; brands may face pressure to qualify, and claims about "natural," "clean," or "no artificial" ingredients will draw more scrutiny as definitions get codified.
- School channel is a distinct market. If any product reaches school sales, the school-food bills in Iowa, Nebraska, Tennessee, and elsewhere impose tighter ingredient lists (especially on colors).
- Watch 2027 and Washington. The presenter expects a post-election surge in state bills and notes that whether Congress or FDA sets a national standard will heavily shape the environment. Regulatory monitoring should not lapse after the current session.

# 05-california-compliance-strategies-summary

In one sentence: California runs its own stricter food-additive bans, date-label rules, ultra-processed-food agenda, and a fast-growing Proposition 65 litigation machine that increasingly targets food and beverage companies, so anyone selling into the state needs a California-specific compliance plan rather than relying on federal rules alone.

## What The Session Covered

Two OFW Law litigators walked through why California is a regulatory world of its own and why selling nationally effectively means complying with California. Because of the state's market size, what California requires often becomes the de facto national standard. The session covered five areas: state additive and color bans (AB 418 and the school-food ban), standardized date labeling (AB 660), the emerging ultra-processed-food bills, Proposition 65 liability for food and beverages, and a baby-food testing-disclosure requirement. The bulk of the practical depth was on Proposition 65, including the litigation mechanics that make it expensive and hard to defend, and concrete steps to reduce exposure.

This is a substantive deck, not an outline. Most slides carry real provisions, dates, and dollar figures, though several bills listed are still proposals rather than enacted law.

## Key Points

### Additive And Color Bans

- AB 418 (California Food Safety Act). Passed October 7, 2023; compliance date January 1, 2027. As of that date it is unlawful to manufacture, sell, distribute, hold, or offer for sale any food for human consumption containing four substances: brominated vegetable oil (BVO), potassium bromate, propylparaben, and Red Dye No. 3 (erythrosine). Penalties run up to $5,000 for a first violation and up to $10,000 for each subsequent violation. This is a statewide product ban, not just a labeling rule.
- BVO and Red Dye No. 3 are the two AB 418 substances most relevant to beverages. BVO was historically used in some citrus-flavored sodas as an emulsifier to keep flavor oils suspended; Red Dye No. 3 is a synthetic red colorant. Both must be out of any product sold in California by January 1, 2027.
- AB 2316 (school additive ban). Passed September 28, 2024; compliance date December 31, 2027. Bans six synthetic dyes from "competitive foods" sold during school hours: Blue No. 1, Blue No. 2, Green No. 3, Red No. 40, Yellow No. 5 (tartrazine), and Yellow No. 6 (Sunset Yellow). Narrow exceptions exist for fundraising sales held off-premises or on-premises at least 30 minutes after the school day ends. This is scoped to school sales, not the general retail market.
- AB 2034 (proposed, introduced February 17, 2026; proposed compliance July 1, 2027). Would require companies to notify the California Department of Public Health (CDPH) before using a new additive or ingredient, submitting the same evidence required for a federal GRAS notification, with CDPH then licensing or declining it. Would also require manufacturers to give CDPH a list of products sold in the state and identify ingredients not individually named on the label.

### Date Labeling (AB 660)

- Passed September 28, 2024; compliance date July 1, 2026. Standardizes date-label wording to cut consumer confusion and food waste.
- Date labeling stays voluntary; the law does not require a date. But if a date is used, it must use standardized terms: "BEST if Used By" or "BEST if Used or Frozen By" (acronym "BB" on small packages) for quality, and "USE by" or "USE by or Freeze by" (acronym "UB") for safety.
- The term "sell by" is prohibited. Non-compliance can be a misdemeanor.

### Ultra-Processed Foods

- AB 1264 (enacted in 2025; the deck lists September 12, 2025). Defines "ultra-processed food" as any food or beverage containing ingredients with specified technical effects (emulsifiers, stabilizers, colors, flavors, flavor enhancers, non-nutritive sweeteners) that also either contains high amounts of saturated fat, sodium, or added sugar, or contains a non-nutritive sweetener or other specified substances (for example hydrogenated starch hydrolysates and sucralose). Directs CDPH to define "ultra-processed foods of concern" and "restricted school foods" by June 1, 2028, with school phase-out/compliance milestones running through July 1, 2035. The presenters flagged that there is no scientific consensus on what "ultra-processed" means.
- AB 2244 (proposed, introduced February 19, 2026). Would create a state "Non-Ultraprocessed Certified" seal administered by CDPH, with a public list of certified products and enforcement against misuse of the seal.

### Proposition 65 Mechanics

- Proposition 65 is a 1986 ballot-passed right-to-know law. It bans nothing and sets no ingredient limits; it requires a "clear and reasonable warning" before knowingly exposing someone to a listed chemical known to cause cancer or reproductive harm. The list grew from about 30 chemicals in 1987 to roughly 1,000.
- The state agency OEHHA sets "safe harbor" exposure levels for some, but not all, listed chemicals. Two safe-harbor types: the No Significant Risk Level (NSRL) for carcinogens, set so exposure causes no more than one excess cancer case per 100,000 people over a 70-year lifetime; and the Maximum Allowable Dose Level (MADL) for reproductive toxicants, set at 1/1000 of the level with no observed birth-defect risk. MADLs tend to be very low.
- Burden-shifting favors plaintiffs. The plaintiff only has to show the chemical is present in the product. The burden then shifts to the defendant to prove the exposure is below the safe harbor. Where no safe harbor exists, the defendant must affirmatively prove the level is too low to cause harm, which requires expert exposure analysis and is expensive.
- Every party in the supply chain can be liable, though retailers get special treatment. A "naturally occurring" defense exists (the chemical occurs naturally rather than from man-made contamination such as pollution), per Brown v. Tri-Union Seafoods (2009).
- Food-specific case law: Environmental Law Foundation v. Beech-Nut (2015) bears on how exposure should be assessed (frequency of consumption by actual eaters, and lot-to-lot variation). Some of the healthiest foods carry the most exposure risk.
- Litigation is largely a private-enforcement, settlement-driven business. A plaintiff must serve a 60-day notice plus a Certificate of Merit before suing; a defective notice removes standing. Most notices never become lawsuits and most simply disappear rather than settle, but settlements at the notice stage are lucrative for plaintiffs (no court oversight of fees, low work, a roughly 20x fee-to-penalty ratio).
- Food's share of Prop 65 activity has grown sharply. Food-related notices rose from about 363 in 2017 to over 1,200 in 2022, and food climbed from roughly 12 percent of all notices in 2017 to around 35–38 percent by 2020–2022. The chemicals driving food notices are lead, cadmium, mercury, inorganic arsenic, and acrylamide. Lead is by far the most frequent. The presenters noted USDA-labeled meats are generally not a Prop 65 concern.

### Defensive Steps Against Prop 65

- Use warnings broadly, drafted to be "clear and reasonable" (warning fatigue is not necessarily a defense for food).
- Test products every year.
- Build supplier and buyer contracts with indemnity provisions.
- Structure settlements to be broad in release but narrow in admitted liability.

## What It Means For A Beverage Business

- AB 418 is the most immediate hard deadline. By January 1, 2027, any beverage sold in California cannot contain BVO or Red Dye No. 3 (or potassium bromate or propylparaben). For a sparkling-beverage maker, the practical action is to confirm with R&D and suppliers that no flavor system, emulsifier, or colorant carries BVO or Red No. 3, and to reformulate before the deadline if any does. Because California reformulation usually drives national reformulation, this likely becomes a single nationwide spec change rather than a California-only SKU.
- The synthetic dyes in the AB 2316 school ban (including Red No. 40 and the yellows) are a watch item if any product is sold into school channels, and a reputational signal even outside schools given the broader push against synthetic dyes.
- AB 660 date labeling has a July 1, 2026 deadline and touches packaging directly. If any product carries a date code or "best by" style statement, the wording must conform to the standardized terms and must not say "sell by." This is a label-copy review, not a formulation change.
- The ultra-processed-food bills are the longer-horizon strategic risk. Non-nutritive and artificial sweeteners (including sucralose) and added-sugar thresholds are named triggers, which is directly relevant to both diet/zero-sugar and sweetened sparkling lines. Nothing requires action now for general retail, but product and marketing should track how CDPH defines "ultra-processed foods of concern" and watch the proposed "Non-Ultraprocessed Certified" seal as a possible future marketing lever or competitive pressure.
- Proposition 65 is an ongoing liability exposure rather than a one-time fix. Beverages can pick up trace lead, cadmium, or arsenic from ingredients such as fruit, botanicals, flavor concentrates, or water sources. Because a plaintiff only needs to show a listed chemical is present and the burden then falls on the company to prove it is below a safe harbor, the practical defenses are annual testing, supplier indemnity language, and appropriate warnings. This is a recurring cost-of-doing-business-in-California item for operations, quality, and legal, not a label project that ends.
- The litigation is bounty-driven and settlement-oriented. Most notices target the easy money of a quick settlement. The defensive posture is documentation: test data showing levels under safe harbors, contracts that push risk back to suppliers, and warning language ready to deploy.

# 06-aligning-on-food-claims-summary

In one sentence: A food claim is only safe to put on a label when the science supports it and the law can defend it at the same time, so R&D, marketing, and legal must agree on the claim at the concept stage, not after the package is designed.

Note on the source: This is a real content deck (20 slides), not a bare outline, but the detailed legal case law and enforcement examples were clearly delivered verbally on top of the slides. Also note: the prompt names the legal presenter as Anita Talati; the deck itself shows Ashish Talati of Talati Law Firm. The summary follows the deck.

## What The Session Covered

The session is built around one image: three teams, one label. R&D supplies the evidence, marketing supplies the message, and legal supplies the risk read. A claim is "defensible" only where all three overlap. The presenters' through-line is that the documentation behind a claim (the "dossier") is the actual product, and if a claim is not documented it is not defensible.

The deck frames 2026 as a "convergence year" where several federal and state changes land at once: the new voluntary "healthy" definition, a projected front-of-pack nutrition rule, a synthetic dye phase-out, an unfinished definition of "ultra-processed food," and the closing of a "GRAS loophole." It then walks through the specific claim areas where R&D, marketing, and legal most often fall out of sync, and closes with three governing principles.

Topics covered: the "bench to label" translation gap; protein and satiety claims; gut health and immunity claims; express, implied, and net-impression claims; influencer and AI-generated marketing; the "healthy" rule transition; the front-of-pack nutrition box; added-sugar and sugar-alternative claims; "natural" and "clean"; bioengineered (BE) disclosure; food dyes; sustainability claims; "GLP-1 friendly" labeling; and a rapid-fire tour of current litigation themes.

## Key Points

- The defensible claim sits where R&D evidence, marketing message, and legal risk overlap. One label, three teams. The recurring failure is getting these teams in a room too late, over a finished mock-up, instead of at the concept stage.
- The "bench to label" gap: most claim failures skip a rung. A claim should clear four checks before it earns a label spot: the ingredient has a real mechanism; the product contains an effective, achievable dose; the ingredient is bioavailable in that actual food matrix (not just in isolation); and the outcome is clinically meaningful in the target group. Matrix and dose are the most common failure points. Ingredients behave differently in a finished food than in a study capsule.
- Protein claims live on a spectrum. "Excellent source, 20 g protein per serving" is a defensible nutrient-content claim. "Supports muscle recovery" is a structure/function claim. "Keeps you full longer" is a functional satiety claim and the hardest to substantiate. Quality matters, not just quantity; DIAAS accounts for digestibility in a food matrix, and extrapolating from isolated-protein studies is incorrect.
- Gut and immunity claims conflate distinct evidence bases. A study on one probiotic strain does not substantiate a different strain, let alone a category claim like "probiotics support gut health." Prebiotic, fiber, and "supports gut health" are three different standards. Immunity surrogate markers (NK cells, sIgA) are measurable but rarely predict a real clinical outcome, which is exactly what regulators and the National Advertising Division (NAD) ask about. DSHEA supplement-style structure/function language does not transfer to conventional foods.
- Net impression is the legal standard, not the literal words. FTC and courts judge the total takeaway a reasonable consumer gets from the whole package: express words plus implied cues (imagery, color, a green leaf, certifications, icons). Fine print on the back panel does not cure a deceptive front-panel impression; disclaimers must be proximate and prominent to the claim they qualify. The FTC prefers human randomized controlled trials (RCTs) for health-related claims; the FTC Health Products Compliance Guidance is the operative document.
- If the brand benefits, the brand owns the substantiation. FTC Endorsement Guides apply to all influencer content, and undisclosed material connections are a recurring 2025-26 enforcement target. AI-drafted copy can fabricate plausible claims and even hallucinate study citations, which is direct false-advertising exposure. Every external channel (creator posts, AI-drafted emails, paid social, influencer kits) must run through the single claims register, not just the package.
- The "healthy" rule changed. The new definition (effective April 28, 2025; mandatory compliance February 25, 2028) replaces nutrient thresholds with a food-group model. Fortification can no longer rescue a poor nutritional profile. Whole foods like nuts, seeds, salmon, olive oil, and water gain the claim; fortified white bread, sweetened cereals and yogurts, and fortified fruit punch lose it. The strategic move is to audit every "healthy"-claimed SKU now, before the 2028 deadline becomes a crisis.
- The front-of-package "Nutrition Info Box" (final rule projected by this deck for spring 2026) would put saturated fat, sodium, and added sugars on the principal display panel rated Low/Med/High if finalized. The projected compliance window is roughly three years after the effective date for $10M+ food sales, four years for smaller firms. The strategic point: this redesign and the "healthy" SKU audit could overlap, so brands should plan for one redesign window, not two.
- Sugar is the most politically charged nutrient on the 2026 agenda. The latest Dietary Guidelines took a hard line ("no amount of added sugars or non-nutritive sweeteners is recommended or considered part of a healthy or nutritious diet"), and that language will surface in litigation. There is a claim-language gradient: "no added sugar" is defined and auditable; "naturally sweetened" is implied with no standard; "guilt-free" is a pure net-impression litigation magnet. Allulose, monk fruit, stevia, and sucralose each carry distinct regulatory status and consumer-perception risk.
- "Natural" and "clean" are commercially powerful and legally exposed; neither has a settled federal definition, so the plaintiffs' bar effectively sets the standard. The most common "natural" failure is not a bad label decision; it is a scale-up ingredient or processing-aid swap at the supplier level that nobody routes back to claim review, making a "no artificial ingredients" claim quietly false. Rule: every ingredient change order must land on the claims register, not just the spec sheet. Bioengineered (BE) disclosure under NBFDS is a compliance obligation but is not a safe harbor for parallel "natural" marketing; class actions target that exact pairing.
- "GLP-1 friendly" is an unregulated category everyone is shipping. FDA has no standard; USDA approved label use for two major brands (Conagra, Nestle) while simultaneously stating there is no regulatory standard for the term. The risky posture is the drug-adjacent inference "GLP-1 friendly." The defensible posture is a specific, measurable nutrient claim ("20 g protein, 10 g fiber per serving") that describes what the food is, not what drug it complements.
- The three takeaways: substantiation is both a science and a legal decision and must satisfy both bars at once; regulatory belongs at the concept gate, not at mock-up review; and the dossier is the product, so every channel, reformulation, and external message must trace to a substantiated entry in the claims register.

## What It Means For A Beverage Business

For a non-alcoholic sparkling beverage company like Talking Rain, this session maps almost directly onto the marketing surface of a product like Sparkling Ice.

- Sweetener and sugar messaging is the front line. "Zero sugar," "no added sugar," and sweetener choices (sucralose, stevia, allulose, monk fruit) sit exactly where the deck flags the highest litigation and perception risk. "No added sugar" is defensible and auditable. Softer phrases like "naturally sweetened," "guilt-free," or "better for you" drift into the net-impression zone where there is no standard and the plaintiffs' bar sets the bar. Keep sugar messaging in the defined, auditable language.
- Flavor and color claims carry hidden supply-chain risk. "Naturally flavored," "no artificial colors," and "natural" on a sparkling water imply something specific. The deck's core warning is that a supplier-level swap (a new flavor system, a color or processing-aid change) can quietly falsify a "no artificial" claim, and the brand only finds out when a class action arrives. Every ingredient or supplier change order on a beverage must route back to claim review, not just to the spec sheet. With the synthetic dye phase-out (Red 40, Yellow 5/6, Blue 1, and others phasing out by end of 2026, plus state warning-label laws), any color reformulation is a claims event.
- Functional and wellness positioning needs its own evidence. Beverages increasingly carry "immunity," "energy," "hydration," "gut health," or added-vitamin claims. The deck is blunt: a nutrient-content claim ("excellent source of vitamin C") does not substantiate a broader functional benefit, surrogate markers rarely satisfy regulators or NAD, and category-level claims exceed strain- or ingredient-specific evidence. Functional claims need separate, stronger substantiation than nutrient claims.
- Net impression means the whole can is the claim. Imagery, color, a leaf icon, a "spring water" cue, and certifications all build implied claims that the legal team is judged on, even when the literal words are accurate. A disclaimer in fine print will not rescue a bold front-of-can impression. Marketing should treat the full visual package, not just the headline copy, as the claim under review.
- Two label-change workstreams may converge and should be planned together. The front-of-package Nutrition Info Box (saturated fat, sodium, added sugars rated Low/Med/High if finalized) and any "healthy" claim audit could force packaging redesigns in the same window. Aligning them avoids paying twice for design, agency review, retailer approval, and print.
- Influencer and AI copy are brand liability. Sparkling beverages lean heavily on creator and social marketing. If a creator or an AI-drafted email makes a claim, the brand owns the substantiation, and undisclosed paid relationships are an active FTC enforcement target. All external channel copy should pass through the same claims register as the label.
- "GLP-1 friendly" is tempting and risky for beverages. If the brand wants to ride that trend, the defensible path is a specific nutrient claim (protein, fiber, calorie content), not a drug-adjacent inference.

# 07-qa-regulatory-collaboration-summary

In one sentence: This session lays out how the Quality Assurance and Regulatory functions divide labor, where they overlap, and a repeatable five-step framework for making joint decisions when ingredients, labels, or formulas go wrong.

## What The Session Covered

The deck defines two distinct food-company functions and shows how they work together to keep products both legal and safe. Regulatory owns what a product must legally say and claim. QA owns what the process must consistently do to make that product safely. The talk presents seven collaboration skills, a five-step decision-making framework, and three worked case studies (excess sauce on a school-nutrition item, an incoming ingredient label that did not match spec, and a clean-label reformulation request from Marketing). The speaker is Belinda Davis, Director of Food Safety/Quality Assurance (FSQA) Labeling and Regulatory Compliance at Tyson Foods.

This is a complete slide deck, not an outline. The case studies are presented as discussion prompts rather than fully resolved answers, so the resolutions are implied by the framework, not spelled out.

## Key Points

- Two roles, one mission. Regulatory answers "what must the product legally say and claim to be compliant" — laws, labeling, claims, approvals, and defending decisions to regulators. QA answers "what must the process consistently do to make the product safely" — food-safety systems, supplier and process verification, execution, validation, and monitoring. Both exist to protect the consumer and the brand.
- The overlap is where the work happens. The two functions share specifications, ingredients and allergens, change management, and consumer protection. The framing line: Regulatory defines what must be true on paper; QA ensures it is true every time the product runs.
- Seven collaboration skills. (1) Shared risk mindset — always ask "if this fails, what is the consumer risk?" (2) Early and continuous engagement from ideation through launch. (3) Clear role clarity to prevent rework. (4) Communication and translation between regulatory language and process reality. (5) Specification discipline — one source of truth, no undocumented changes. (6) Mutual respect — neither team is "just a gatekeeper" or "just execution." (7) Audit-ready documentation that is defensible months or years later.
- Five-step decision framework (the "collaboration spine"). Trigger (what changed or went wrong) - Risk (consumer safety, regulatory exposure, business continuity) - Decision Rights (who decides what and when) - Controls and Proof (records and release gates that show the process worked) - Prevent Recurrence (lock the fix in through standard work and change control).
- Decision rights are the crux. The hard case (Case Study 2) is a production line scheduled to run when an incoming ingredient label does not match the approved spec and no substitute is available. Stopping the line is costly; running it on a non-conforming ingredient needs a named approver and documentation in hand before start. The framework forces the company to decide in advance who holds that authority.
- Documentation is protection, not bureaucracy. Every decision must be documented, reproducible, and defensible later. Specification changes require documented review from both functions, and everyone must know what data is final versus in progress.

## What It Means For A Beverage Business

- The QA/Regulatory split applies directly to a sparkling-beverage operation. Regulatory governs your Nutrition Facts panel, ingredient declarations, allergen statements, and front-of-pack claims (for example "zero sugar," "natural flavors," "no artificial sweeteners"). QA governs whether the line actually delivers what the label promises every batch — carbonation, fill, flavor dosing, sweetener levels, water quality, and supplier verification.
- The "can we run?" scenario is a beverage reality. When an incoming flavor, acid, sweetener, color, or CO2 supply arrives with a spec or label mismatch and a production run is already scheduled, the cost of stopping the line is real. You need a pre-agreed decision-rights map and a non-conforming-material hold-and-release process before the moment of pressure, not invented on the spot.
- Clean-label and reformulation pressure is named explicitly (Case Study 3). When Marketing wants to drop ingredients that "aren't easily recognizable" — preservatives, stabilizers, certain acids or flavor carriers — removing them can change shelf life, microbial stability, flavor, and label claims. QA and Regulatory both need to be at the table early, before a launch date is committed.
- Early engagement saves money. Looping QA and Regulatory in at ideation prevents late redesigns, blown timelines, and label do-overs. For a fast-moving brand, the cost of a recall, a mislabel, or a stalled launch dwarfs the cost of a few early review meetings.
- Keep one specification as the single source of truth. Version control on specs, documented dual-function sign-off on changes, and clear "final vs. in progress" status prevent the most common and expensive failure: production running to an out-of-date or unapproved spec.
- Build an audit trail now. Records that are documented, reproducible, and defensible years later protect the brand in an FDA inspection, a retailer audit, or a consumer-complaint investigation.

## Plain-English Glossary

| Term | Plain meaning |
| --- | --- |
| QA (Quality Assurance) | The function that makes sure the process consistently makes a safe product that matches its spec, batch after batch. |
| Regulatory | The function that makes sure the product is legal — labeling, claims, and rules — and can be defended to authorities. |
| Specification (spec) | The single agreed-upon recipe and standard for a product or ingredient; the source of truth everyone works from. |
| Non-conforming ingredient | An incoming material that does not match its approved spec or label; cannot be used without a documented review and approval. |
| Decision rights | A pre-agreed map of who has authority to decide what, and at which stage. |
| CAPA | Corrective and Preventive Action; the discipline of fixing a problem and locking in changes so it never recurs. |
| Change management | The controlled process for reviewing and approving any change to a product, ingredient, or spec before it takes effect. |
| Clean label | A marketing goal of removing ingredients consumers see as artificial or unrecognizable; has food-safety and shelf-life consequences. |
| Release gate | A checkpoint that product must pass — with proof — before it can ship. |
| Audit-ready documentation | Records complete enough to prove the process worked as intended, defensible months or years later. |

# 08-ai-in-food-safety-summary

In one sentence: A food-science data scientist walks through where AI actually
helps food safety today, why the technology matters less than the
organizational readiness to use it, and how to adopt it without
over-engineering.

## What The Session Covered

This is a full slide deck (22 slides), not an outline. Claire Zoellner,
Director of Data Science at iFoodDS (a Seattle-area food safety, quality, and
traceability software firm), surveys the landscape of artificial intelligence
in food safety. The talk is structured as a tour: it defines the categories of
AI, maps them to concrete food-safety uses, presents adoption data and
barriers, and closes with a practical framework for deciding what to automate
and how far.

The deck opens by framing AI as a quality-control and product-innovation
lever, not a job-cutting tool (citing a 2024 Goldman Sachs framing). It then
traces a food-safety "transformation" timeline (2004 to 2020) tied to
regulatory milestones such as the FDA Food Safety Modernization Act and the
FDA New Era of Smarter Food Safety blueprint, arguing those changes already
pushed the industry toward the digital data foundations AI needs.

The core of the talk groups AI uses into four food-safety functions: refining
risk communication (chatbots and augmented reality for training, translating
complex topics, drafting food safety plans, identifying regulatory
requirements), facilitating risk assessment (computer vision and sensors that
shift testing from periodic batches to continuous monitoring), enabling risk
management (predictive analytics and simulation that recommend actions, citing
FDA PREDICT for import screening and horizon-scanning platforms), and a worked
example of Listeria control using agent-based modeling and a "digital twin"
of a processing facility.

The deck then covers the state of adoption (FAO experts estimate 5-plus years
to widespread impact), the 2026 view of AI maturity (from basic machine
learning up to agentic AI), the size of the opportunity, the barriers, and two
decision frameworks for responsible adoption.

## Key Points

- AI is a stack, not one thing. The 2026 model runs from AI and machine
  learning (turning data into decisions), to neural networks (pattern
  detection), to generative AI (producing content and code), to AI agents
  (executing complex tasks autonomously), to agentic AI (automating whole
  processes). Each layer builds on the one below it.
- The direction of travel is from narrow, single-task statistical models toward
  end-to-end "decision execution" — systems that don't just flag a problem but
  recommend or take the next action.
- The highest-value applications are not pure AI; they combine AI with
  food-safety context and human expertise. AI alone underperforms a model that
  is grounded in domain knowledge.
- Continuous monitoring is the practical near-term shift: sensors, IoT devices,
  and computer vision moving facilities from batch sampling to real-time
  detection of contamination and SOP compliance.
- Regulatory work is an explicit use case. The deck lists "identify relevant
  regulatory requirements," "write food safety plans," and "enterprise horizon
  scanning platforms" as things AI already assists with — the regulatory-monitoring
  angle is named directly.
- Adoption is slower than the hype. FAO experts put widespread, meaningful
  impact 5-plus years out; digital food twins are high-potential but currently
  low-feasibility; blockchain traceability is a 15-year horizon.
- The barriers have not changed: data accuracy (bad inputs poison every
  downstream insight), data privacy and security, regulatory risk (liability
  when incidents occur), ethics and transparency, internal governance and
  acceptable-use policy, and cost (infrastructure, talent, data fees, ongoing
  maintenance).
- "AI readiness" is a measurable thing. Zoellner's own framework scores three
  overlapping factors: organizational buy-in and a real business problem, IT
  support and data literacy, and food-safety process maturity. Valuable AI
  solutions sit where all three overlap; missing any one produces "irrelevant,"
  "disparate," or "fleeting" solutions.
- Decide how far to automate deliberately. The deck imports a 10-level scale of
  automation (from "the computer offers no help" up to "the computer decides
  everything and ignores the human") and pairs it with a warning about
  "over-complexification" — past a point, added complexity yields declining
  return on investment, so favor "innovation by subtraction."
- Coordination across the supply chain requires shared data standards, scalable
  use cases (including regulatory and legal ones), available data (synthetic
  data can augment real data), and validation studies run in a sandbox or
  phased rollout.

## What It Means For A Beverage Business

- The regulatory-monitoring use case is real and near-term. The deck explicitly
  names AI for identifying regulatory requirements, drafting food safety plans,
  and "horizon scanning." For a beverage company tracking ingredient,
  additive, and labeling rules across jurisdictions, this validates building or
  buying AI-assisted regulatory monitoring — but as an assist that a person
  reviews, not an autonomous decision-maker.
- Most of the food-safety examples are produce and microbial (Listeria,
  Salmonella, fresh-cut lettuce). A sparkling-beverage operation has a
  different and generally lower microbial risk profile, so the contamination-detection
  and digital-twin examples translate loosely, not directly. The transferable
  ideas are continuous sensor monitoring of the production line, automated data
  capture for traceability, and predictive flagging of issues before they
  become incidents.
- Readiness beats technology. The repeated message — organizational change and
  investment matter as much as or more than the model — means a beverage
  company should first confirm it has clean, structured data and a specific
  business problem before buying an AI tool. AI on messy data produces
  confident wrong answers.
- The barriers map onto brand and legal exposure. Data accuracy and regulatory
  risk are the two that matter most for a consumer beverage brand: an AI that
  misreads a label requirement or a spec creates liability, not efficiency.
  Any AI output touching compliance or labeling needs human review and an
  audit trail.
- Watch the cost and over-complexity trap. The "declining return on investment"
  warning is a direct caution against adopting AI for its own sake. The test the
  deck offers — what value are we bringing and can it be sustained — is the
  right screen for any R&D, operations, or marketing AI pilot.
- "Governed and auditable access" is the enterprise pattern. The deck points to
  scoped AI platforms on your own data, skilled agents tied to your workflows,
  and access that is governed and logged. For a beverage company, that argues
  against ad-hoc use of public chatbots for proprietary formulation or
  regulatory work, and toward a controlled enterprise deployment.

## Plain-English Glossary

| Term | Plain meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Narrow AI / narrow model | AI built for one specific task (e.g. spotting one defect), not general reasoning. |
| Machine learning (ML) | Software that learns patterns from data instead of being explicitly programmed. |
| Generative AI | AI that produces new content — text, images, code (e.g. ChatGPT, Claude). |
| AI agent | AI that carries out a multi-step task on its own toward a goal, not just one answer. |
| Agentic AI | AI that automates an entire process end to end, chaining many agent actions. |
| Digital twin | A software simulation of a real facility used to test scenarios without touching the plant. |
| Agent-based modeling | Simulating many individual actors (people, bacteria) to see how a system behaves over time. |
| Computer vision | AI that interprets images or video — e.g. detecting microcolonies or checking compliance. |
| IoT (Internet of Things) | Networked sensors and devices that report data automatically (temperature, etc.). |
| ROI (return on investment) | Whether the value gained exceeds what the effort and money cost. |
| AI readiness | How prepared an organization is — data, skills, buy-in — to actually use AI well. |
| Horizon scanning | Systematically watching for emerging risks, rules, or signals before they hit. |
| Synthetic data | Artificially generated data used to fill gaps when real data is scarce. |
| Human-in-the-loop | A person reviews or approves the AI's output before action is taken. |

# 09-law-talk-summary

In one sentence: This is the closing session of the conference, a live,
two-lawyer "what to watch next year" conversation that runs through the major
moving pieces in U.S. food regulation, delivered almost entirely out loud
against an outline-only slide deck.

## What The Session Covered

This is a header-only, outline-style deck. Every slide is a topic title with
a photo or cartoon and no body text. There are no bullet points, no data,
and no policy detail on the slides themselves. The substance was delivered
verbally by the two presenters; the deck is just a running agenda for that
talk. As a result, this summary reflects what each topic header signals, not
specific claims made in the room.

Two practical notes on the source. First, the slides credit the speakers as
Chris Forgues, Assistant General Counsel for Food, Feed, and Products Law at
Cargill, and Tony Pavel, a partner at the law firm Keller and Heckman in
Washington, DC. The conference program lists the session under different
first names (Tracy Pavel and Cathy Forgues); treat the on-slide names as the
people who actually presented. Second, the title is a play on the old
Regis-and-Kathie-Lee morning show, signaling an informal, banter-style
session rather than a technical deep dive.

The agenda, slide by slide, was: FDA and FSIS leadership changes; a MAHA
update; GRAS, ultra-processed foods, and front-of-pack labeling; FDA
post-market review of the preservatives BHA and BHT plus rosemary extract in
Canada; state and federal legislative activity; FSIS relocation and other
developments; and Product of USA origin labeling. These themes overlap
heavily with Sessions 2 through 5 of the conference, which covered the same
GRAS, additive-review, MAHA, and state-law topics in detail. This session is
the round-up, not the primary source for any one of them.

## Key Points

- FDA and FSIS leadership changes. New political leadership at the Food and
  Drug Administration (FDA, the federal agency that regulates most packaged
  food and beverages) and at the Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS,
  the USDA agency that regulates meat and poultry) means new priorities and
  new people deciding what gets enforced. Leadership turnover usually predicts
  shifts in what the agency pushes on.

- MAHA update. MAHA stands for "Make America Healthy Again," the health-policy
  agenda associated with the current administration and Health Secretary
  Robert F. Kennedy Jr. The "what's cooking in that tallow" slide nods to its
  signature themes: pressure on synthetic additives, artificial dyes, seed
  oils, and a general "fewer industrial ingredients" message.

- GRAS, UPF, and FOP. Three acronyms to know. GRAS ("Generally Recognized As
  Safe") is the pathway by which a company can add an ingredient without
  formal FDA pre-approval if qualified experts agree it is safe; FDA is
  signaling tighter scrutiny of that self-affirmation route. UPF means
  "ultra-processed foods," a category regulators are starting to define and
  potentially target. FOP means "front-of-pack" labeling, a proposed
  standardized nutrition box on the front of the package highlighting sugar,
  sodium, and saturated fat.

- FDA post-market review of BHA and BHT. BHA and BHT are common synthetic
  preservatives (antioxidants that keep fats and oils from going rancid).
  "Post-market review" means FDA is re-examining the safety of ingredients
  already on the market, not just new ones. The rosemary-extract-in-Canada
  reference points to a natural antioxidant being used as a replacement and a
  cross-border angle on what counts as an approved alternative.

- State and federal legislative activity. The "I'm Just a Bill / Confusion"
  cartoon flags a flood of new bills at both the state and federal level,
  many overlapping or conflicting. The footnote ("individual bills may be
  counted more than once") signals a high-volume, fragmented landscape where
  states are moving faster than Congress.

- FSIS relocation and other developments. Operational upheaval at the meat-
  and-poultry agency, including office relocation, which can slow rulemaking
  and inspections.

- Product of USA. Origin labeling for meat: new requirements govern when a
  product can carry a "Product of USA" claim. Relevant mainly to meat and
  poultry, less directly to beverages, but part of a broader origin-claims
  trend.

## What It Means For A Beverage Business

- The acronyms in the middle of this deck are the ones that touch a sparkling-
  beverage portfolio most directly. GRAS scrutiny, ultra-processed-food
  definitions, and front-of-pack labeling all bear on sweeteners, flavors,
  colors, preservatives, and how a low- or zero-sugar drink gets characterized
  on the front of the can.

- Front-of-pack labeling is the single most concrete near-term item. If a
  standardized FOP nutrition box is finalized, every package gets a front-of-
  can redesign and the sugar, sodium, and saturated-fat call-outs become a
  marketing and reformulation question, not just a compliance one.

- The MAHA "fewer synthetic ingredients" pressure flows toward artificial
  colors, artificial sweeteners, and synthetic preservatives. Even where the
  rules do not change immediately, retailer and consumer expectations move
  ahead of the rules. Watch the additive-review list.

- The pace of state legislation matters more than any single federal action.
  A patchwork of state ingredient bans and labeling rules can force a national
  brand to reformulate or relabel to the strictest state, well before FDA acts.

- Leadership turnover and agency relocation mean uncertainty and slower, less
  predictable federal guidance. Plan for ambiguity; do not assume a clear
  federal answer will arrive on schedule.

- BHA, BHT, and origin labeling are lower priority for a sparkling-water
  business. They matter most for fat-containing and meat products. Note them,
  but they are not the headline for this category.
