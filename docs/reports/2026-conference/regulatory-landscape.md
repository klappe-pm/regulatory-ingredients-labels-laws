# regulatory-landscape

The 2026 compliance problem is no longer federal or state but both at once, and the response is a single cross-functional ingredient and claims playbook built for readiness rather than prediction.

## Summary

Amaru J. Sanchez, Counsel at Akin Gump, presented a strategy session on building a cohesive labeling and ingredient compliance program in a colliding federal-state environment. He laid the statutory foundation of FDA authority and NLEA preemption, mapped FDA's 2026 priorities against the rising state patchwork, detailed the legal collision points, and offered an eight-step unified strategy with discussion scenarios. The deck carries an attorney-advertising disclaimer.

## Key Points

On federal authority, a food is misbranded if its labeling fails statutory standards and may not enter interstate commerce. Mandatory label elements include the statement of identity, ingredient declaration in descending order of predominance, net quantity, manufacturer or distributor name and place, and the Nutrition Facts panel required since the 1990 NLEA. Claims are regulated by category, with nutrient-content claims conforming to FDA descriptors, health claims requiring authorization or authoritative statements, and structure/function claims permitted under different standards for foods and supplements. Ingredient safety runs through the 1958 Food Additives Amendment, which requires pre-approval unless GRAS, and FALCPA's 2004 allergen labeling.

The complexity lives in the state layer. NLEA section 403A preempts state requirements that are not identical to federal ones across standards of identity, defined labeling categories, nutrition labeling, and nutrient-content and health claims, but Sanchez emphasized the critical exception that safety warnings are not preempted, citing NLEA section 6(c)(2), and that states may petition FDA for exemption under section 343-1(b) on three conditions. He mapped FDA's 2026 Human Foods Program priorities across food chemical safety, including GRAS reform from voluntary to mandatory, post-market chemical reviews, contaminant action levels, natural color work, supplement oversight, and caffeine and allergen labeling, and across nutrition and labeling, including front-of-package, the "healthy" claim, added sugar, sodium targets, and online grocery labeling, with three-to-four-year compliance windows.

The state patchwork is concrete. Texas SB 25 targets 44 ingredients with a January 2027 label deadline and has drawn a granted injunction in the Western District of Texas, with challenges on First Amendment compelled speech, federal preemption, vagueness, and the Commerce Clause. New York S1239F pairs ingredient bans on Red 3, potassium bromate, and propylparaben with a mandatory self-affirmed GRAS disclosure database and awaits the governor's signature. Other developments include Louisiana SB 14's QR-code disclosures and school restrictions, Arizona HB 2164's school UPF ban, California AB 1264's UPF definition and school restrictions, West Virginia's retail ingredient ban with a preliminary injunction, Utah and Virginia school bills, and 17-plus states exploring SNAP purchase limits. The key trend is divergence, with states variously banning ingredients, requiring disclosure, or restricting distribution channels, forcing companies to comply across multiple regulatory models simultaneously. The collision points are preemption scope, First Amendment compelled-speech doctrine running from Zauderer rational basis to strict scrutiny, dormant Commerce Clause interstate-burden analysis, and the underlying question of who decides safety among FDA, state legislatures, and courts.

The recommended response is an eight-step unified strategy: build one master ingredient and claims inventory rather than multiple trackers, classify regulatory status, map triggers by function rather than ingredient name, create a label-change control gate, ensure GRAS and post-market file readiness, segment products into reformulate, relabel, restrict, or defend, update supplier contracts and attestations, and align litigation, regulatory, science, and communications. Four discussion scenarios stress-test the playbook across a multi-state Red 40 and BHA snack, a self-affirmed GRAS beverage in New York, a 2027 "healthy" claim and packaging launch, and a "no artificial colors" claim using not-yet-listed natural colorants.

## Takeaways

The five closing principles are to maintain one inventory rather than multiple trackers, to treat GRAS documentation as a regulatory asset that protects against both federal and state risk, to plan simultaneously for reformulation and legal uncertainty since challenges may delay laws but ingredient scrutiny is permanent, to use label-change moments strategically by coordinating front-of-package, "healthy," state warnings, and voluntary updates, and to aim for readiness rather than prediction. This breakout is the strategic counterpart to the MAHA and state-initiatives sessions, converting their landscape into an operating playbook.
