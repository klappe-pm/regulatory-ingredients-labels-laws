# food-label-conference-2026-beverage-briefing

## Who This Is For

This briefing is written for the people who build and sell the product, not the
people who read the statutes. If you work in R&D, brand, marketing, operations,
sales, or leadership at a non-alcoholic beverage company like Talking Rain, this
document tells you what changed in 2026 food regulation, why it is happening,
and what your team should do about it. Every regulatory term is defined in plain
language the first time it appears, and there is a glossary at the end.

It is built from the nine sessions of the 38th Annual Food Label Conference
(June 1-2, 2026). The detailed per-session notes live in
`docs/reports/conference-source-summaries/`. This is the connective document
that ties those findings together for the business.

One caveat the lawyers made repeatedly: this is a plain-language digest of a
conference, not legal advice. Confirm anything that touches a live label against
current source text and your own counsel before you act on it.

## The Whole Story In One Paragraph

For years, a beverage company could design one label and sell it in every state,
because federal law set a single national standard. That is ending. In 2026 the
federal government is running its biggest food-labeling reform in a generation,
and at the same time individual states are passing their own ingredient and
labeling laws faster than ever. Both land on the same can at the same time. The
honest takeaway from the conference was not a prediction about which rule wins.
It was a posture: stop trying to guess the future, and instead get organized
enough to move fast when any of these rules lands. The company that keeps one
clean master list of its ingredients and claims, knows which products are
exposed to which rule, and can change a formula or a label on a planned schedule
will win. The company that finds out from a lawsuit will not.

## The Two Forces Colliding

### Force One: Federal Reform Under MAHA

MAHA stands for "Make America Healthy Again." It is the health agenda associated
with Robert F. Kennedy Jr., who now runs the U.S. Department of Health and Human
Services, the parent agency of the FDA. Its goals are to reduce diet-related
chronic disease (especially in children), remove additives and dyes it considers
harmful, push "real food" over heavily processed food, and demand more
transparency on labels.

Two practical warnings came up again and again:

- FDA leadership is unstable. The commissioner changed (Marty Makary out, Kyle
  Diamantas in as likely acting), but the Human Foods Program, the part of FDA
  that actually runs food policy, has stayed steady under a long-time veteran.
  Policy direction has been consistent even while the nameplate changes.
- This administration announces major policy outside the normal legal process.
  Several speakers warned that big changes now arrive as press announcements
  rather than formal rules. You have to watch what officials say, not only what
  gets published in the Federal Register.

### Force Two: The State Patchwork

States are no longer waiting for Washington. The numbers tell the story: in 2023
there were 3 ingredient bills in 2 states. In 2025-2026 there were more than 140
bills across 42 states, with new laws on the books in 13 states and more than 100
different ingredients targeted somewhere. California leads and effectively writes
the template the other states copy.

The problem for a business is not any single law. It is that fifty states can
each write a slightly different rule, and "not identical to federal" is exactly
the kind of difference that forces a separate label or a separate formula.

## The Five Changes Reshaping The Label

These five federal threads came up across nearly every session. Each one can
also appear in a stricter state version.

| Change | What it is, in plain terms | Why a beverage team cares |
|---|---|---|
| GRAS overhaul | GRAS means "Generally Recognized As Safe." For decades a company could decide on its own that an ingredient was safe and use it without telling FDA. FDA has proposed closing that self-certification path and is re-reviewing older additives. | Any ingredient you rely on under self-affirmed GRAS now needs a clean, audit-ready safety file. Preservatives like BHA and BHT are early review targets. |
| Synthetic dye phase-out | FDA is pushing a voluntary phase-out of six petroleum-based color dyes by the end of 2026, with Red No. 3 moving faster. "No artificial colors" claims are allowed now under enforcement discretion. | Flavored and colored sparkling drinks may need to reformulate to natural colors. A color swap is also a claims event, not just a recipe change (see Claims below). |
| Ultra-processed food (UPF) definition | There is no official definition of "ultra-processed" yet. FDA and USDA are jointly writing one. Once it exists, it becomes a lever for warnings, school bans, and marketing pressure. | Sweetened and heavily formulated beverages are likely to land inside whatever definition emerges. This is a watch item, not an immediate deadline. |
| Front-of-package labeling (FOP) | A new box on the front of the package that rates Saturated Fat, Sodium, and Added Sugars as Low, Medium, or High. MAHA has floated turning it into a green/yellow/red "traffic light." | Added sugar is the most likely "High" trigger on a sweetened drink. This reshapes the front panel, so coordinate it with any other redesign. |
| The new "Healthy" rule and claims | "Healthy" now means a product fits a food-group model, not just hitting nutrient thresholds. Adding vitamins to an unhealthy base no longer rescues the claim. Effective April 2025, mandatory by February 2028. | If any product carries or implies "healthy," it has to be re-checked against the new definition before the 2028 deadline. |

## California Is The One To Watch First

Because this project starts with U.S. federal plus California, California gets
the most attention. It has two distinct kinds of risk.

### California's New Ingredient Laws

| Law | What it does | Date that matters |
|---|---|---|
| AB 418 (California Food Safety Act) | Bans brominated vegetable oil (BVO), potassium bromate, propylparaben, and Red Dye No. 3 from any food or drink sold in California. Penalties up to $5,000 first violation, $10,000 after. | January 1, 2027 |
| AB 660 | Standardizes date labels: only "BEST if Used By" and "USE by" are allowed; "sell by" is banned. This is a packaging-copy change, not a formula change. | July 1, 2026 |
| AB 2316 | Bans six synthetic dyes from foods sold through school channels. | December 31, 2027 |
| AB 1264 and AB 2244 | Ultra-processed food rules, including a proposed voluntary "Non-Ultraprocessed Certified" seal. These name non-nutritive sweeteners such as sucralose. | CDPH defines terms by June 1, 2028; AB 2244 introduced February 2026 |

For a beverage company, BVO and Red No. 3 in AB 418 are the concrete near-term
items. If either is in a formula sold in California, it has to be out before
January 1, 2027. Confirm with R&D and suppliers now, because reformulation takes
time.

### Proposition 65: A Different Kind Of Risk

Proposition 65 is a 1986 California "right-to-know" law. It does not ban
anything. It requires a warning if a product exposes someone to one of roughly
1,000 listed chemicals above a safety threshold. The reason it is dangerous to a
business is the structure:

- A private plaintiff only has to show the chemical is present. They do not have
  to prove harm.
- The burden then shifts to your company to prove the exposure is below the
  official "safe harbor" level. Some chemicals have no safe harbor level at all.
- It is bounty-driven and settlement-oriented. Food-related notices grew from a
  few hundred a year to more than 1,200 a year, and legal fees in these cases
  often run around twenty times the actual penalty.

The chemicals that drive food cases are heavy metals (lead, cadmium, arsenic,
mercury) and acrylamide. For beverages, the realistic exposure routes are trace
heavy metals carried in by ingredients such as botanicals, colors, fruit
concentrates, or certain minerals. The defenses are unglamorous and ongoing:
annual testing, supplier indemnity and attestation, and clear warnings where
needed.

## Why Beverages Are In The Crosshairs

Not everything at the conference was about drinks. But several themes hit
non-alcoholic beverages directly.

- Added sugar is the single most politically charged nutrient of 2026. It is the
  most likely front-of-package "High" rating and the clearest target of school
  and SNAP restrictions.
- Sugar alternatives are the next battleground. Allulose, monk fruit, stevia,
  and sucralose are all under labeling scrutiny. Federal dietary guidance now
  includes the line that "no amount of added sugars or non-nutritive sweeteners
  is recommended," and that language will show up in lawsuits and marketing
  fights.
- SNAP purchase restrictions are spreading. Twenty-three states have approved
  waivers that block soda, sweetened drinks, and energy drinks from SNAP
  benefits, with effective dates rolling out 2026 to 2028. The wording in some
  states is broad enough ("sweetened carbonated," for example) that it could
  catch a zero-sugar sparkling water depending on how it is read. This deserves a
  state-by-state look at the actual product list.
- Caffeine and energy positioning carry their own track. Voluntary federal
  caffeine-labeling guidance is in progress, and Canada's supplemented-foods
  regime adds cautionary statements and caffeine limits.
- Color and preservative swaps (dyes, BHA, BHT, BVO) hit beverages directly, and
  every one of them is both a formula change and a claims change.

There is an upside here, not just risk. A zero-sugar, dye-free, clean-ingredient
line is well positioned for exactly where the rules are heading. A favorable
front-of-package rating and an honest "no artificial colors" claim can be brand
assets. The catch is that the same product's use of non-nutritive sweeteners is
the part most likely to draw a labeling challenge, so the win and the risk sit on
the same can.

## Claims: The Most Important Marketing Lesson

The session on aligning R&D, marketing, and legal had the sharpest practical
message for a brand team.

- A claim is only safe where three things overlap: the science supports it, the
  marketing wants it, and the legal risk is acceptable. The proof file behind a
  claim is the actual product. Treat the claims register as a real asset, not
  paperwork.
- Courts and the FTC judge the "net impression" of the whole package, including
  images, colors, icons, and certifications. Fine print on the back cannot fix a
  misleading message on the front.
- The most common way companies get caught is a quiet supplier change. A
  supplier swaps an ingredient or a color, the spec sheet updates, but nobody
  re-checks the "natural" or "no artificial" claim on the front of the package.
  Every change order has to route back through claims review, not just R&D.
- Unsettled words are traps. "Natural," "clean," "guilt-free," and "GLP-1
  friendly" have no fixed legal definition, which means a plaintiff's lawyer gets
  to define them for you. Default to specific, measurable claims that describe
  what the product is.
- Anything an influencer or an AI tool writes is the brand's liability. If a
  creator says it, the brand owns it. A hallucinated study citation in AI-drafted
  copy is false-advertising exposure.

## How The Operating Side Should Respond

Two sessions covered how the company should organize internally.

- Regulatory and Quality Assurance are one mission with two jobs. Regulatory
  decides what must legally be true on the label. QA makes sure it is true on
  every production run. They overlap on specifications, ingredients, allergens,
  and any change. The core control is a single, versioned specification with
  sign-off from both functions and no undocumented changes.
- Decide the hard calls before the line stops. The conference framed every
  incident as a five-step spine: Trigger, then Risk, then Decision Rights, then
  Controls and Proof, then Prevent Recurrence. The most beverage-relevant example
  is the "can we run?" moment, when an incoming ingredient does not match spec and
  a production line is scheduled. Decide in advance who is allowed to authorize
  running with a non-conforming ingredient, so the answer is a policy, not a
  panic.

## What AI Can And Cannot Do Here

The AI session was deliberately sober, which is useful given this project's own
goal of automating regulatory monitoring.

- Readiness matters more than the tool. AI applied to messy data produces
  confident wrong answers. Clean, structured data and a specific business problem
  have to come before any tool purchase.
- Keep a human in the loop on anything with legal liability. The highest value
  comes from pairing AI with real food-safety and regulatory expertise, not from
  letting it decide.
- The honest timeline for broad, meaningful impact is five-plus years. The
  barriers (data accuracy, privacy, liability, governance, cost) have not changed.
- Regulatory monitoring and horizon scanning were named as legitimate AI use
  cases. That validates the direction of this project, as a reviewed assistant
  that flags changes for a human, not an autonomous system that acts on its own.

## What Talking Rain Should Actually Do

A short, ordered list the business can act on:

1. Build one master list of every ingredient and every claim across the
   portfolio, and map each ingredient by what it does (color, sweetener,
   preservative) rather than only by name. When a new ban hits a function, you
   want to find every affected product in minutes.
2. Run the California near-term check now: any product sold in California
   containing BVO or Red No. 3 must be reformulated before January 1, 2027, and
   date-label wording must comply with AB 660 by July 1, 2026.
3. Pull a Proposition 65 exposure read on heavy metals and acrylamide across
   ingredients, and confirm supplier testing and indemnity are in place.
4. Audit every front-of-package and "healthy" or "clean" style claim against the
   new federal definitions, and plan one coordinated label redesign window rather
   than several piecemeal changes.
5. Make every supplier or formula change trigger a claims-register review, with
   regulatory brought in at the concept stage, not at final artwork.
6. Do a state-by-state read of which products are caught by SNAP and school
   restrictions, paying attention to broad wording that could reach zero-sugar
   lines.
7. Treat regulatory monitoring as a governed, human-reviewed process, and keep
   the underlying ingredient and claims data clean enough that automation can sit
   on top of it later.

## Glossary

| Term | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| MAHA | "Make America Healthy Again," the federal health agenda led by RFK Jr. as head of HHS. |
| HHS | U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, the parent agency of FDA. |
| FDA | Food and Drug Administration, regulates about 80 percent of the U.S. food supply. |
| GRAS | "Generally Recognized As Safe," the status that lets an ingredient be used without full premarket approval. |
| FOP | Front-of-package labeling, the new front-label nutrition rating box. |
| UPF | Ultra-processed food, a category being defined to drive future labeling and restrictions. |
| Prop 65 | California's 1986 right-to-know law requiring warnings for listed chemicals. |
| Safe harbor | The exposure level under Prop 65 below which no warning is required. |
| NLEA preemption | The federal rule that normally blocks states from setting different labeling requirements, with a carve-out for safety warnings. |
| BVO | Brominated vegetable oil, an emulsifier used in some flavored drinks, banned in California as of 2027. |
| Net impression | The legal standard that judges the overall message of a package, not just the literal words. |
| SNAP | The federal food-assistance program; several states now block sweetened drinks from it. |
